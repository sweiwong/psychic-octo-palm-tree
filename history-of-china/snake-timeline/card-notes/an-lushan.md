The An Lushan Rebellion was the civil war that began on 16 December 755, when An Lushan (安禄山 Ān Lùshān, c. 703–757), commander of three of the [[id:tang|Tang]] empire's ten frontier armies, marched south from Fanyang (范阳 Fànyáng) against the court of Emperor Xuanzong (玄宗 Xuánzōng, r. 712–756). Tradition treats it as the shock that ended a golden age. It took the standing provincial armies the Tang had already built on its frontier and spread them across the whole empire.

## The armies Xuanzong built

The office of military commissioner (节度使 jiédùshǐ) was created for border defence early in the eighth century and grew under Xuanzong into ten standing commands holding 486,900 men between them while the capital garrisons decayed. An Lushan held three of them, Fanyang, Pinglu (平卢 Pínglú) and Hedong (河东 Hédōng), with about 183,900 soldiers, close to two-fifths of the frontier army. The rotating militia (府兵制 fǔbīngzhì) had been abolished in 749, six years before the rising, and the border was held by long-service professionals whose pay and promotion ran through their commander. From about 747 the chancellor Li Linfu (李林甫 Lǐ Línfǔ, d. 752–753) filled the commands with generals of non-Chinese origin to keep rivals out of his own office. An Lushan, a frontier market broker who spoke six non-Chinese languages, was the son of a Turkic mother and, on most accounts, a Sogdian (粟特 Sùtè) father, though the surname An came from a stepfather and scholars differ over how much it proves.

## The march on the capitals

Luoyang (洛阳 Luòyáng) fell thirty-three days after the march began, and in February 756 An Lushan declared himself emperor of Great Yan (大燕 Dà Yān). The court executed its two generals at Tong Pass (潼关 Tóngguān), ordered their replacement out to attack, and lost its field army in early July. Xuanzong fled Chang'an (长安 Cháng'ān) for Chengdu (成都 Chéngdū) on 14 July 756; the next day, at the Mawei post station (马嵬驿 Mǎwéiyì), his escort killed the chancellor Yang Guozhong (杨国忠 Yáng Guózhōng) and forced Yang Guifei (杨贵妃 Yáng Guìfēi) to hang herself. In August his heir took the throne at Lingwu (灵武 Língwǔ) as Suzong (肃宗 Sùzōng, r. 756–762).

The rebel leadership then destroyed itself. An Lushan was murdered in January 757 at his own son's instigation, and the war passed to Shi Siming (史思明 Shǐ Sīmíng), who was strangled by his son in 761. Tang recovery depended on cavalry from outside the empire. Uyghur (回纥 Huíhé) squadrons broke the rebel line outside Chang'an in November 757 and outside Luoyang weeks later, paid with three days' plunder of Luoyang and a horse trade priced at forty rolls of silk a head. The last rebel claimant killed himself in February 763.

## The settlement of 763

The court bought peace by confirming four former rebel commanders in provinces of their own, among them Tian Chengsi (田承嗣 Tián Chéngsì) at Weibo (魏博 Wèibó). Three of these became the three garrisons of Hebei (河北三镇 Héběi sān zhèn), which kept their armies, forwarded no tax to Chang'an and treated the commissionership as heritable property. The court extended that arrangement rather than dismantle it. David Graff counts the ten frontier commands of 755 becoming more than forty provinces across the whole empire, each placed to check its neighbours, and that structure held until the dynasty ended. With the western armies drawn east, the Tibetan empire (吐蕃 Tǔbō) took the corridor to Central Asia and held Chang'an itself for a fortnight in November 763. The Shence Army (神策军 shéncèjūn), raised against Tibet in 754 and cut off from its base, ended as the court's central force under eunuch command.

## What the registers count

Registered households fell by about two-thirds between the last pre-war count and 764, and registered individuals by almost the same proportion, so the average household on the books shrank only from 5.94 people to 5.77. Mass death thins households; it does not remove them whole at the rate it removes individuals. Du You (杜佑 Dù Yòu, 735–812), who compiled the empire's figures a generation later, thought even the pre-war registers were short by some four million households.

## Why the rebellion matters

Within fifty years the defining literary treatment of the war was a love story. Bai Juyi (白居易 Bái Jūyì, 772–846) wrote the "Song of Everlasting Sorrow" (长恨歌 Cháng Hèn Gē) about Xuanzong, Yang Guifei and Mawei in 806, and the poem carried the war through [[id:yuan-drama|Yuan drama]] to the Qing stage. The record from inside reads differently: [[id:li-bai-du-fu|Du Fu]] (杜甫 Dù Fǔ, 712–770) on conscription and forced partings, and the requiem Yan Zhenqing (颜真卿 Yán Zhēnqīng) drafted in 758 for a nephew killed at Changshan (常山 Chángshān), corrections left in.

Sogdians fought on both sides, and the lesson later drawn about foreign generals has its own history. One Sogdian officer asked Suzong in 757 to release him from the surname An, took the imperial surname instead, and held the western frontier for a decade as Li Baoyu (李抱玉 Lǐ Bàoyù, 703–777). Chen Yinke (陈寅恪 Chén Yínkè) explained Hebei's autonomy in 1943 by the region turning culturally non-Chinese; Shao-yun Yang, re-reading the key ninth-century inscription in 2018, reads that language of barbarism as an insult aimed at disobedience.

What the war destroyed is still argued. The standard account of the Tang-Song transition (唐宋变革 Táng-Sòng biàngé) makes the medieval aristocracy a casualty of the eighth century. Nicolas Tackett, working from more than 30,000 excavated tomb epitaphs (墓志铭 mùzhìmíng), argues that the great clans came through the rebellion intact, kept the senior offices for another century, and were destroyed only in the killing that followed [[id:huang-chao|Huang Chao's]] sack of the capitals after 880. On that reading the Tang lost control of its armies and its registers in 755, and its old society in 880.
