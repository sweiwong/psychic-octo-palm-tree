The Qin Dynasty (秦朝 Qín Cháo) ruled from 221 to 206 BCE. During those fifteen years, it created an empire governed through appointed officials and common standards. Qin itself was much older, one of several kingdoms that fought for power during the Warring States period (战国时期 Zhànguó Shíqī). Its rulers built a state that could register households, collect grain, recruit soldiers and move supplies across long distances. Those abilities helped Qin defeat its rivals. They also allowed Qin Shi Huang (秦始皇 Qín Shǐhuáng), the First Emperor (始皇帝 Shǐ Huángdì), to place heavy demands on the people he ruled.

## From a rival kingdom to the First Emperor

Qin had existed as a state under the [[id:catalog-R_ZHOU|Zhou Dynasty (周朝 Zhōu Cháo)]] for centuries before it became an empire. Its rise took generations. Reforms traditionally associated with [[id:shang-yang|Shang Yang (商鞅 Shāng Yāng)]] in the fourth century BCE rewarded farming and military achievement, tightened the registration and supervision of households, and extended the authority of appointed officials. Collective responsibility made groups of families answerable for one another's conduct. These policies weakened some inherited privileges while directing more people and resources toward the state.

Between 230 and 221 BCE, King Ying Zheng (嬴政 Yíng Zhèng) of Qin [[id:unification|conquered the six rival states]] of Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan and Qi. He then adopted the title First Emperor, claiming a rank above the kings who had ruled the competing states. He governed from Xianyang (咸阳 Xiányáng), near present-day Xi'an (西安 Xī'ān).

Victory did not make the conquered regions identical. They had their own customs, loyalties and administrative practices. Qin's officials had to turn military conquest into everyday government. This slower work depended on local records, transport networks and people who could carry out orders far from the capital.

## Governing conquered territories

The Qin court extended a commandery-county system (郡县制 jùnxiàn zhì) across the empire. The central government appointed officials to govern territorial units instead of granting large hereditary kingdoms to members of the ruling family. This gave the emperor a more direct claim over taxation, law and compulsory service, though officials still depended on local knowledge and cooperation.

Li Si (李斯 Lǐ Sī), the First Emperor's chief minister, is closely associated with the use of small seal script (小篆 xiǎozhuàn) as a standard for official writing. This policy did not erase every regional form of writing at once. It gave officials a shared written system for government documents. Qin authorities also [[id:catalog-E_QIN_2|standardized weights and measures (度量衡 dùliànghéng)]] and promoted common coinage, including the round banliang coin (半两钱 bànliǎng qián) with its square hole. Rules for cart axles helped vehicles follow established road tracks.

Common standards made goods and records easier to compare across regions. They could support trade and helped officials assess taxes, record supplies and transmit instructions. Excavated documents show many offices handling the routine work that turned imperial commands into local action.

## Roads, walls and the First Emperor's tomb

Qin drew soldiers, conscripted workers and people serving penal sentences into military campaigns and construction. They built roads, worked on palaces and extended defences along the northern frontier. Earlier kingdoms had already built walls there. Qin joined, rebuilt and adapted some of them as a precursor to the [[id:qin-great-wall|Great Wall (长城 Chángchéng)]]. Most of the stone and brick structures familiar today belong to much later periods, especially the Ming dynasty.

The Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor (秦始皇陵 Qín Shǐhuáng Líng) placed similar demands on labour. The wider burial complex covers more than 50 square kilometres. Its best-known remains are pits containing thousands of life-sized [[id:catalog-C_QIN|terracotta warriors (兵马俑 Bīngmǎyǒng)]], horses and chariots. Workshops assembled the figures from modular parts and finished them by hand. Their varied faces and equipment show organized production and careful workmanship. Most scholars do not treat them as portraits of individual Qin soldiers.

Farmers digging a well uncovered fragments from the terracotta army in 1974. Archaeologists have since excavated parts of the surrounding complex, while the central burial mound remains unopened. A traditional account describes a tomb chamber with rivers of mercury and a model of the heavens. Since the chamber has not been entered, that description cannot confirm what survives inside.

## Law, punishment and contested memory

Qin government placed great weight on written rules, official responsibility, rewards and punishment. Later scholars grouped several texts and thinkers under the name Legalism (法家 Fǎjiā). They offered different arguments about law, authority and the conduct of officials, so the label does not describe one uniform Qin doctrine.

In 213 BCE, an order associated with Li Si targeted selected private copies of historical and philosophical writings. Technical works and official archives were exempt. The familiar claim that Qin burned every book outside a narrow state canon exaggerates the order's scope.

Traditional histories also say that the First Emperor had 460 scholars buried alive the following year. The details remain disputed, including who the victims were and what punishment they received. The story became part of a larger portrait of Qin as the enemy of learning, remembered in the phrase "burning books and burying scholars" (焚书坑儒 Fénshū Kēngrú).

The court moved powerful families from conquered regions toward the capital, reducing their ability to rebuild local bases of power. Compulsory labour, military service and punishment gave Qin government a harsh presence in ordinary lives. Later Han writers had political and moral reasons to emphasize that harshness. Excavated laws and administrative records provide another view of how Qin offices worked in practice.

## Rebellion and collapse

The First Emperor died in 210 BCE while travelling through the eastern part of the empire. Transmitted histories say that the court officials Zhao Gao (赵高 Zhào Gāo) and Li Si concealed his death and helped place his younger son Huhai (胡亥 Hú Hài) on the throne as Qin Er Shi (秦二世 Qín Èrshì), the Second Emperor. Succession struggles and killings at court soon weakened the government.

The first major revolt began in 209 BCE under Chen Sheng (陈胜 Chén Shèng) and Wu Guang (吴广 Wú Guǎng). Other rebellions followed. Xiang Yu (项羽 Xiàng Yǔ), a noble from the former state of Chu, and Liu Bang (刘邦 Liú Bāng), a local official of modest background, later emerged as leading competitors. In 207 BCE, Liu Bang entered the Qin capital region and Ziying (子婴 Zǐyīng), the last Qin ruler, surrendered. The dynasty is conventionally dated to 206 BCE, when rival leaders divided its territory. Fighting between Liu Bang and Xiang Yu continued in the Chu-Han Contention (楚汉战争 Chǔ-Hàn Zhànzhēng).

## What survived under Han

Liu Bang defeated Xiang Yu and became emperor in 202 BCE, founding the [[id:han|Han Dynasty (汉朝 Hàn Cháo)]]. Han rulers retained and adapted many Qin institutions. They governed some territories through appointed officials in commanderies and counties, while other regions remained kingdoms held by members of the imperial family. They also revised laws, taxes and demands for service.

Official writing, measures, coinage and territorial administration continued to develop after Qin. Later dynasties repeatedly used the imperial title and governed through appointed officials, but each changed the system to meet its own political needs. Qin's ruling house disappeared in 206 BCE. Under Han, commanderies stood alongside kingdoms, and institutions associated with Qin became part of a different settlement.

## Note

221–206 BCE is the conventional range for the Qin imperial dynasty; the Qin state existed for centuries before unification. Ziying surrendered in 207 BCE. Accounts of the book restrictions, executions and succession crisis come mainly through later transmitted histories and require caution. Qin's policies also varied in their local reach and implementation.
