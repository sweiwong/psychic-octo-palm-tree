The Ming (明 Míng) dynasty ruled China from 1368 to 1644 after overthrowing [[id:yuan|Mongol rule]]. Its emperors built [[id:catalog-C_BEIJING_MING|Beijing’s]] (北京 Běijīng) imperial palace and much of today’s Great Wall (长城 Chángchéng). Growing trade and population supported a flourishing market for porcelain, silk and books before rebellion and conquest ended the dynasty.

## Zhu Yuanzhang founds the Ming

Zhu Yuanzhang (朱元璋 Zhū Yuánzhāng), the founding Hongwu emperor (洪武 Hóngwǔ, r. 1368–1398), came from a poor farming family. After famine and disease killed his parents, he entered a Buddhist monastery and spent time begging for food. In 1352 he joined the [[id:red-turbans|Red Turban rebels]] (红巾军 Hóngjīnjūn), one of several movements fighting the Mongol-led Yuan (元 Yuán) dynasty.

The Yuan government was losing control as disasters and rebellion spread. Zhu built an army, recruited advisers and captured Nanjing (南京 Nánjīng) in 1356. From this base, he defeated rival rebel leaders. In January 1368 he proclaimed the Ming dynasty. His forces took the Yuan capital later that year, and the Mongol emperor fled north. Further campaigns brought remaining territories under Ming control.

Hongwu wanted farming households to provide dependable taxes and service to the state. He registered land and families, and assigned hereditary duties to soldiers and craftsmen. A military family, for example, had to keep supplying a soldier across generations.

He also concentrated power in his own hands. In 1380 he executed his chief minister and [[id:hongwu-chancellor|abolished the chancellorship]], leaving the ministries directly answerable to the emperor. Purges killed thousands of officials and their associates. Later rulers relied on senior secretaries to manage the paperwork, but the founder’s system gave no single minister an independent position above the others.

## Yongle’s capital and ocean voyages

Hongwu’s death in 1398 brought his grandson to the throne. The new emperor soon faced a rebellion led by his uncle Zhu Di (朱棣 Zhū Dì), an experienced commander based in the north. After a civil war, Zhu Di seized Nanjing in 1402 and became the Yongle emperor (永乐 Yǒnglè, r. 1402–1424).

Yongle made Beijing the main capital in 1421. There he built the Forbidden City (紫禁城 Zǐjìnchéng), the palace compound used by China’s emperors for the next five centuries. The [[id:sui-grand-canal|Grand Canal]] (大运河 Dà Yùnhé) carried grain from the south to feed the northern court and its troops. Nanjing remained a secondary capital.

Yongle also sent fleets into the Indian Ocean under [[id:zheng-he|Zheng He]] (郑和 Zhèng Hé), a Muslim [[id:catalog-E_MING_EUNUCH_1|eunuch]] who served as a naval commander. Seven expeditions between 1405 and 1433 visited ports across Southeast Asia and as far as East Africa. They carried gifts and brought foreign envoys to the Ming court, where rulers could acknowledge the emperor’s authority through tribute missions.

The last voyage sailed after Yongle’s death. Later emperors stopped these costly expeditions. Chinese merchants continued sailing overseas, although [[id:ming-maritime-conflict|government restrictions]] often put their trade outside the law.

## The northern frontier

Expelling the Yuan court had not removed the Mongol armies beyond China’s northern border. Ming rulers fought campaigns, negotiated trade and built defences to deal with them. In 1449, a Ming emperor led an expedition north and was [[id:tumu-crisis|captured at Tumu]] (土木 Tǔmù). The court installed another emperor, and the dynasty survived the defeat.

Much of the familiar brick-and-stone Great Wall dates from the Ming. Watchtowers passed warnings along the frontier, while garrisons guarded fortified passes. Earlier dynasties had built walls too, but the Ming defences were extensive new works and reconstructions. They required continuing supplies, repairs and soldiers.

The coast posed different problems. Restrictions on private sea trade encouraged smuggling, and armed raiders attacked coastal settlements. The government relaxed the overseas trade ban in 1567. Throughout the dynasty, officials had to decide which contacts to permit and which to fight.


## Silver, crops and a larger population

By the later Ming, merchants linked farming regions, workshops and cities through busy markets. Some communities specialised in cotton cloth, others in silk or sugar. Women spun and wove at home, supplying textiles that merchants sold far from the villages where they were made.

China’s population grew substantially. Estimates place it at roughly 60–80 million near the dynasty’s beginning and 150–200 million toward its end, although some estimates are higher. These are reconstructions rather than reliable census totals. Late in the dynasty, new crops from the Americas, including maize and sweet potatoes, allowed farmers to cultivate some land poorly suited to established crops.

Foreign buyers paid for Chinese silk and porcelain with silver. Supplies came from Japan and, through [[id:manila-silver|Manila in the Philippines]], from mines in the Spanish Americas. This trade connected Chinese producers to markets across the Pacific as well as the Indian Ocean.

Silver also became central to taxation. Reforms extended under [[id:zhang-juzheng|Zhang Juzheng]] (张居正 Zhāng Jūzhèng) in the late sixteenth century combined many taxes and labour obligations into payments in silver. Officials could then pay workers instead of summoning households for compulsory service. Farmers who sold produce for copper coins still had to obtain the silver needed for their taxes.

## Books, learning and new ideas

The civil service examinations (科举 kējǔ) again became the main route into government. Families invested in teaching boys the classical texts on which they would be tested. Provincial quotas helped spread opportunities beyond the richest and best-educated regions, although success remained difficult.

Printers sold books to a growing market. Long novels drew on stories that performers had told for generations. [[id:ming-journey-west|Journey to the West]] (西游记 Xīyóu jì) followed a Buddhist pilgrim and his magical companions, including the Monkey King, on their journey to India. Romance of the Three Kingdoms (三国演义 Sānguó yǎnyì) turned the struggles after the [[id:han|Han]] (汉 Hàn) dynasty into an extended tale of rival rulers and warriors.

[[id:ming-wang-yangming|Wang Yangming]] (王阳明 Wáng Yángmíng) challenged the idea that moral understanding depended on years of scholarly investigation. He argued that people possessed an innate knowledge of right and wrong, which they had to put into practice. Knowing how to honour one’s parents, for example, meant acting accordingly in daily life.

Contacts with European missionaries brought exchanges in astronomy, mathematics and geography. Matteo Ricci worked with Chinese scholars, and his [[id:ricci-world-map|Chinese-language world map of 1602]] included the Americas. Some educated readers welcomed this learning while rejecting the missionaries’ religious claims.


## Porcelain for the court and the world

Jingdezhen (景德镇 Jǐngdézhèn) supplied porcelain to the imperial court and to buyers at home and overseas. Workshops divided the work of shaping, decorating and firing vessels among specialised workers. Imperial orders demanded particular shapes and designs, and pieces that failed inspection could be deliberately smashed.

Blue-and-white wares had cobalt decoration beneath a clear glaze. Potters also made brightly coloured enamelled pieces. A surviving jar from the Jiajing reign (嘉靖 Jiājìng, 1522–1566) combines blue dragons with stylised characters for longevity. It was made at Jingdezhen in an imperial workshop. Other kilns produced wares for sale across the world.


## Rebellion and the Qing conquest

In the early seventeenth century, the Ming faced mounting military costs while drought, famine and epidemics devastated communities. Tax revenues fell, soldiers went unpaid, and rebel armies gained recruits. The court also fought an expanding Manchu state in the northeast.

In 1644, [[id:li-zicheng-beijing|Li Zicheng]] (李自成 Lǐ Zìchéng) captured Beijing with a rebel army. The Chongzhen emperor (崇祯 Chóngzhēn) killed himself. The Ming commander Wu Sangui (吴三桂 Wú Sānguì) then allied with the Manchus against Li. Their forces defeated the rebels, and the Manchu-led [[id:qing|Qing]] (清 Qīng) took the capital.

The conquest continued after 1644. Members of the Ming ruling family maintained rival courts in the south, collectively known as the [[id:southern-ming|Southern Ming]] (南明 Nán Míng). The last of these emperors was killed in 1662. The Qing kept Beijing as their capital and continued to govern from the Forbidden City.
