The Yuan (元 Yuán) dynasty was founded by Kublai Khan in 1271. Its Mongol rulers conquered southern China in 1279 and governed a reunited China until 1368. Yuan China was connected to trade across Asia and produced celebrated plays, paintings and blue-and-white porcelain, while Mongols retained privileges over their conquered subjects.

## Kublai founds the Yuan

Kublai inherited a war of conquest that had begun before his birth. His grandfather, Genghis Khan, united the Mongols under his leadership in 1206 and launched campaigns across Asia. After Genghis died, his successors destroyed the [[id:jurchen-jin|Jin]] (金 Jīn) dynasty in 1234 and took control of northern China. The [[id:song|Song]] (宋 Sòng) continued to rule the south.

Kublai became great khan in 1260 after a disputed succession. He wanted the wealth of southern China to support his armies, court and nobles. Taking its cities required fleets and siege weapons, as well as cavalry. Chinese soldiers, shipbuilders and engineers helped the Mongols fight across rivers and break through fortified towns.

In 1271, Kublai gave his state the Chinese dynastic name Yuan. He adopted Chinese court ceremonies and government offices to rule the lands his family had conquered. His capital, [[id:catalog-C_BEIJING_YUAN|Dadu]] (大都 Dàdū), was built at present-day Beijing (北京 Běijīng). He also kept a summer court at Shangdu (上都 Shàngdū), where hunting and life on the grasslands remained part of imperial routine.

Kublai was both a Mongol khan and an emperor of China. The government he built preserved Mongol authority while drawing on Chinese officials and institutions to collect taxes and administer the country.

## Conquest, expansion and rival khans

The Yuan declaration came eight years before the conquest of Song was complete. After a long siege, Xiangyang (襄阳 Xiāngyáng) fell in 1273. Its capture opened the way into the south. The Song capital at Hangzhou (杭州 Hángzhōu) surrendered in 1276, and the last Song resistance ended in 1279. Northern and southern China were once again under one dynasty.

Kublai tried to extend his power overseas. Fleets attacked Japan in 1274 and 1281, but both invasions failed. The second fleet suffered devastating storm damage. These campaigns consumed ships, men and money without bringing Japan under Yuan rule.

The other Mongol rulers across Asia did not all obey Kublai. Separate khanates governed territories farther west, including Persia and southern Russia, and rival Mongol princes fought one another. Yuan China belonged to this wider Mongol world, but its emperor did not directly govern every Mongol conquest.

Kublai died in 1294. His successors kept the dynasty in power for another seventy-four years, although struggles within the ruling family repeatedly disrupted the court. Emperors could be removed by coups or assassination. Officials and commanders had to navigate these changes of ruler as well as run the government.

## Government and unequal status

The Yuan retained ministries responsible for taxes, appointments, justice and public works. Provincial offices carried out orders beyond the capital. Mongol overseers helped keep these officials answerable to the ruling group.

A person's background affected their prospects. Mongols enjoyed the greatest privileges. The court also recruited Central Asians and other outsiders, often giving them positions of authority over Chinese subjects. People from the former Jin territories and those from the conquered Song south were treated differently in appointments and law. Southern Chinese generally faced the greatest disadvantages.

The state also assigned many families hereditary occupations. A military household had to supply soldiers; an artisan household owed skilled work to the government. These duties passed to later generations. Families that could not meet them sometimes fled rather than remain registered where officials could find them.

The civil examinations (科举 kējǔ), which had been an important route into Song government, returned in the early fourteenth century. Quotas favoured Mongols and other privileged groups, and examinations filled only a small share of official posts. Educated men also found work as clerks, teachers and doctors. Some refused to serve the conquerors, while others entered government hoping to protect their communities.

## Canals, ports and journeys across Asia

Dadu needed grain from the productive south. The Yuan extended the Grand Canal north to the capital and also transported supplies by sea. Floods and silt repeatedly blocked the canal, so officials had to switch between routes. Keeping the court supplied required ships, repairs and labour on a large scale.

At Quanzhou (泉州 Quánzhōu), foreign merchant communities traded through a busy seaport. Ships carried Chinese ceramics to buyers around the Indian Ocean. The Yuan also continued the use of paper currency inherited from earlier dynasties, including arrangements to exchange old Song notes for Yuan money.

Mongol rulers welcomed merchants and recruited officials from distant lands. Travellers carried knowledge as well as goods: Persian and Indian astronomy and mathematics reached China, while Chinese technologies travelled west. Marco Polo's account introduced European readers to wealthy Chinese cities and the use of paper money.

Some people travelled by choice; others were moved by force. Mongol conquerors took skilled workers from captured cities and sent them far from home to serve their rulers. The same connections that offered merchants new customers could mean compulsory service for an artisan.

[[id:yuan-religions|Religious communities]] also crossed these routes. Buddhists, Muslims and Christians lived under Yuan rule alongside Chinese religious traditions. Kublai especially favoured Tibetan Buddhism. Patronage was unequal, and his support for Buddhists could come at the expense of their Daoist rivals.

## Plays, paintings and porcelain

The Yuan was a major age of [[id:yuan-drama|Chinese drama]]. Plays combined spoken dialogue with songs, commonly arranged in four acts. Their subjects included love, family conflict and injustice. Performances could reach audiences who would never read a literary text.

Guan Hanqing (关汉卿 Guān Hànqīng) wrote The Injustice to Dou E (窦娥冤 Dòu É yuān), about a widow wrongly condemned to death. She confesses to a murder to save her mother-in-law from torture. The play makes the suffering caused by corrupt justice personal and immediate.

Painting offered another form of expression. Zhao Mengfu (赵孟頫 Zhào Mèngfǔ), a descendant of the Song imperial family, became a Yuan official and an influential painter and calligrapher. Some former friends condemned his decision to serve the Mongols. Yuan scholar-painters used brushwork to express personal taste and feeling, and studied older paintings as models. Later collectors eagerly sought their work.

Potters at Jingdezhen (景德镇 Jǐngdézhèn) made [[id:yuan-blue-white|blue-and-white porcelain]] by painting cobalt designs beneath a transparent glaze. The surviving David Vases have dragons around their bodies and elephant-head handles. Their inscriptions date them to 1351 and record a gift to a Daoist temple.

## Rebellion and the Ming takeover

In the dynasty's final decades, floods, famine and epidemics added to the hardships of taxation and compulsory labour. Court disputes weakened the government's response. The [[id:red-turbans|Red Turban rebellions]] (红巾军 Hóngjīnjūn) spread as rebel leaders gathered followers among people who had lost their livelihoods. Forced river work in 1351 helped fuel the unrest.

Zhu Yuanzhang (朱元璋 Zhū Yuánzhāng), a poor young man who had spent time in a Buddhist monastery, joined the rebels. He built his own army and defeated rival leaders. In 1368 he founded the [[id:ming|Ming]] (明 Míng) dynasty, and his forces took Dadu. The Yuan emperor fled north with his court.

The loss of China did not end the ruling family's claims or its power in Mongolia. Yuan rulers and their successors continued there, while the Ming inherited parts of the government they had displaced. Military and artisan families still owed hereditary service under the new dynasty.
