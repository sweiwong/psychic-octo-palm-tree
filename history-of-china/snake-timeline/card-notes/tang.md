The Tang ruled China for close to three centuries, taking power in 618 after the collapse of the [Sui](../index.html?card=sui) (隋朝 Suícháo), which had [reunified the country in 589](../index.html?card=reunification) following almost four hundred years of division. Chinese tradition treats the Tang as the high point of imperial China in both politics and culture, and the dynasty's territorial reach in the 660s was the largest of any Chinese state before the Manchu [Qing](../index.html?card=qing).

## Frontier origins

The ruling Li (李 Lǐ) family came from the militarised northwest frontier, where Chinese officials had intermarried for generations with Turkic and Xianbei (鲜卑 Xiānbēi) steppe nobility, a world shaped by the [Northern Wei](../index.html?card=catalog-R_NS_NORTHERN_WEI). This mattered in practice. Emperor Taizong (太宗 Tàizōng, born Li Shimin 李世民 Lǐ Shìmín, r. 626–649), who took the throne after [[xuanwu-gate|killing two brothers at the Xuanwu Gate]] and forcing his father's abdication, accepted the steppe title Heavenly Khagan (天可汗 Tiān Kèhán) in 630 after defeating the Eastern Turks. He governed as Chinese emperor and as khan of a nomadic confederation at once, and his [[zhenguan-government|Zhenguan era]] became the model of good government invoked by every later dynasty. Tang armies held protectorates deep into Central Asia until defeat by the Abbasids at [[talas|Talas]] (怛罗斯 Dáluósī) in 751 checked further westward expansion.

## Government

Administratively the Tang built on Sui foundations and produced the model that most later dynasties worked from. The [Tang Code](../index.html?card=tang-code) (唐律疏议 Tánglǜ Shūyì) of 653 was the most influential legal document in East Asian history, adopted in modified form by Japan, Korea and Vietnam. Government ran through three departments and six ministries (三省六部 sān shěng liù bù). Officials were recruited partly through written civil service examinations (科举 kējǔ), [begun under the Sui](../index.html?card=sui-examinations), though the great aristocratic clans retained most senior posts throughout the dynasty. Land was distributed under the equal-field system (均田制 jūntiánzhì) and the army raised through rotating militia service (府兵制 fǔbīngzhì), both of which broke down in the eighth century.

## A world capital

[[chang-an|Chang'an]] (长安 Cháng'ān), the capital, held roughly a million people within a walled grid, and both Nara and Heian-kyō in Japan were laid out in imitation of it. Sogdian and Persian merchants, Japanese and Korean students, and Zoroastrian, Manichaean and Nestorian Christian communities all had a presence there, and a stele erected in 781 records the Christian mission. [Buddhism](../index.html?card=catalog-E_BUD_SPREAD) reached its greatest institutional strength, supported by pilgrim-scholars such as [[xuanzang-return|Xuanzang]] (玄奘 Xuánzàng), who travelled to India between 629 and 645 and returned with several hundred texts. Steppe habits persisted in elite society, and [aristocratic women rode horses, hunted and played polo](../index.html?card=tang-women-riding) with a freedom that later dynasties withdrew. Roughly 48,900 poems by some 2,200 Tang authors survive, and the work of [[li-bai-du-fu|Li Bai (李白 Lǐ Bái) and Du Fu (杜甫 Dù Fǔ)]] became the standard against which later Chinese poetry was measured.

## Wu Zetian

[[wu-zhou|Wu Zetian]] (武则天 Wǔ Zétiān), who entered the palace as a concubine of Taizong and later became consort to his son Gaozong (高宗 Gāozōng), governed in his name during his illness and in 690 declared her own Zhou dynasty (周朝 Zhōucháo). She ruled as emperor in her own right until 705, the only woman ever to do so, and expanded examination recruitment against the interests of the established aristocracy.

## The rebellion and after

The [[an-lushan|rebellion of the frontier general An Lushan]] (安禄山 Ān Lùshān), which began in 755 during the long reign of Emperor Xuanzong (玄宗 Xuánzōng, r. 712–756, whose [[kaiyuan-era|Kaiyuan era]] is remembered as the dynasty's peak) and was not suppressed until 763, permanently altered the dynasty. Registered population fell from about 52.9 million in 754 to about 16.9 million in 764, a figure that reflects administrative collapse as much as mortality, though the human cost was enormous by any measure. Afterwards provincial military governors (节度使 jiédùshǐ) held effective autonomy, the court financed itself through a [[two-tax|new twice-yearly tax]] (两税法 liǎngshuìfǎ) from 780, and palace eunuchs controlled the imperial armies while the bureaucracy consumed itself in [factional struggle](../index.html?card=niu-li-disputes). State [suppression of Buddhism](../index.html?card=huichang) under Emperor Wuzong (武宗 Wǔzōng) in 845 closed thousands of monasteries and marked a broader turn against foreign influence. The [rebellion of Huang Chao](../index.html?card=huang-chao) (黄巢 Huáng Cháo) sacked Chang'an in 881, and in 907 the warlord Zhu Wen (朱温 Zhū Wēn) [deposed the last emperor](../index.html?card=tang-end) and founded the [Later Liang](../index.html?card=catalog-R_FIVE_LATER_LIANG) (后梁 Hòu Liáng), opening the [Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period](../index.html?card=five-dynasties) (五代十国 Wǔdài Shíguó).

## The centre of gravity moves south

The rebellion also accelerated a permanent shift in where China's people and wealth sat. Tang power rested on the Guanzhong basin (关中 Guānzhōng) around Chang'an and the Central Plains (中原 Zhōngyuán) around Luoyang (洛阳 Luòyáng), the old Yellow River heartland. A clear majority of households registered in the census of 742 were northern. The wars of the eighth century, and then the devastation of the ninth, drove sustained migration south across the Huai River into the Yangtze (长江 Chángjiāng) valley, and [by the Song the majority of the population lived in the south](../index.html?card=southward-economic-shift). Wet-rice agriculture in the lower Yangtze yielded far larger surpluses than the millet and wheat farming of the north, and the [Grand Canal](../index.html?card=sui-grand-canal) (大运河 Dà Yùnhé), dug at ruinous cost by the Sui, carried that surplus upriver to feed the capitals. After 755 the court survived on southern grain and on a salt monopoly administered from the Huai region, which by the 780s supplied more than half of its cash revenue. Yangzhou (扬州 Yángzhōu), where the canal met the Yangtze, became the wealthiest commercial city in the empire, and Guangzhou (广州 Guǎngzhōu) carried a seaborne trade with Arab and Persian merchants substantial enough that a massacre of foreign residents there during the Huang Chao rebellion was recorded as far away as Iraq. The two-tax reform of 780, which assessed land and property rather than registered adults, was an admission that the old northern system of allotted fields and counted persons no longer described the country.

## Why the Tang matters

Historians describe the passage from the late Tang into the [Song](../index.html?card=song) as the hinge between medieval and early modern China, an argument first framed by the Japanese scholar Naitō Konan and known as the Tang-Song transition (唐宋变革 Táng-Sòng biàngé). The aristocratic clans that had held the senior offices throughout the Tang were broken by the rebellions of the ninth century, and the Song governed instead through a gentry class made by examination. The capital never returned to Chang'an. The Song ruled from Kaifeng (开封 Kāifēng) and later Hangzhou (杭州 Hángzhōu), both chosen for their access to the canal and the southern economy. Political power had followed the grain.

The Tang reputation as a golden age was largely formed after the fact, by writers of the ninth century onward who understood themselves to be living in the aftermath of something greater. Later dynasties, with north China or the whole country under non-Chinese rule for much of the following millennium, came to treat the Tang as the last great native dynasty. That reading sits awkwardly with the frontier and steppe origins of the Tang ruling house itself.
