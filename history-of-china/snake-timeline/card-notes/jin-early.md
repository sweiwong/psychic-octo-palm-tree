The Jin dynasty (晋朝 Jìn Cháo) ruled from 266 to 420. Its western court ended the [[id:three-kingdoms|Three Kingdoms]] (三国 Sānguó) division in 280, but that reunification lasted only about a generation. Civil war within the ruling family destroyed the court's military power in the north. The Eastern Jin survived at Jiankang (建康 Jiànkāng), now Nanjing, through bargains among the imperial house, northern émigré families, established southern lineages and commanders of refugee armies. Their new political order helped establish the [[id:division|divide between northern and southern China]] that lasted until 589.

## From a court takeover to reunification

The Sima family rose inside [[id:catalog-R_WEI|Cao Wei]] (曹魏 Cáo Wèi), the strongest of the Three Kingdoms. In 249, Sima Yi (司马懿 Sīmǎ Yì, 179–251) seized control of the Wei court. His grandson Sima Yan (司马炎 Sīmǎ Yán, 236–290) accepted the last Wei emperor's abdication in 266 and became Emperor Wu of Jin (晋武帝 Jìn Wǔdì). Over the preceding decades, the family had accumulated senior offices and military commands. It also built alliances with powerful landed families whose support helped the Simas replace the Cao ruling house.

Jin inherited Wei's conquest of [[id:catalog-R_SHU|Shu Han]] (蜀汉 Shǔ Hàn) in 263. In 280, Jin armies and a fleet moving down the Yangtze forced [[id:catalog-R_WU|Eastern Wu]] (东吴 Dōng Wú) to surrender. China was again governed by one imperial court for the first time since the [[id:catalog-E_3K_1|end of Han rule in 220]]. The reunified court increasingly linked a son's first office to his father's rank, giving leading families an inherited advantage without granting them permanent control of government. Surviving population registers show how far the tax rolls and administrative system had contracted during the preceding wars. They do not provide a precise death toll.

## The princes turned protection into civil war

Emperor Wu had watched his own family take power through a court coup. To protect the new dynasty from the same fate, he gave imperial relatives regional commands and armies. Several princes governed populous provinces while commanding forces of tens of thousands. After his death in 290, those commands allowed rival relatives to fight for control of his successor, Emperor Hui (晋惠帝 Jìn Huìdì). Empress Jia Nanfeng (贾南风 Jiǎ Nánfēng, 257–300) dominated the early court struggle. The [[id:eight-princes|War of the Eight Princes]] (八王之乱 Bā Wáng zhī Luàn), fought from 291 to 306, grew from palace coups into campaigns between armies led by members of the ruling family.

The policy solved one danger by creating another. The court distributed military power but could not control the relatives who received it. Their war drained the dynasty's money and armies, damaged cities and farms, displaced communities and pulled frontier commanders into struggles at the centre. Xiongnu (匈奴 Xiōngnú), Jie (羯 Jié), Xianbei (鲜卑 Xiānbēi), Di (氐 Dī) and Qiang (羌 Qiāng) soldiers fought within these changing coalitions. They were different peoples with different leaders and aims, rather than one invading force. Han-Zhao armies captured Luoyang (洛阳 Luòyáng) in 311 and [[id:catalog-E_JIN_1|captured Chang'an]] (长安 Cháng'ān) in 316, ending the [[id:catalog-R_JIN_W|Western Jin]] (西晋 Xī Jìn) court. Later histories called the first disaster the Disaster of Yongjia (永嘉之乱 Yǒngjiā zhī Luàn), but their enormous casualty figures are not modern counts.

## A refugee court built through bargains

In 317, Sima Rui (司马睿 Sīmǎ Ruì, 276–323) established a Jin government at Jiankang. He took the imperial title the following year. The [[id:catalog-R_JIN_EASTERN_JIN|Eastern Jin]] (东晋 Dōng Jìn) claimed continuity with the fallen western court, but ruled different territory. Wang Dao (王导 Wáng Dǎo, 276–339) helped persuade established southern families to accept the refugee court. Imperial titles and offices offered prestige, while the new government promised order and protection. Northern émigré families gained land and careers in return for supporting the Sima emperor.

The Wang (王 Wáng), Xie (谢 Xiè), Yu (庾 Yǔ) and Huan (桓 Huán) clans held senior offices, estates and military commands that the emperor needed. Their politics of great families (门阀政治 ménfá zhèngzhì) kept the court working while limiting how directly an emperor could command. Family background opened doors to office, while poetry, calligraphy, philosophical conversation and cultivated behaviour distinguished the highest families from people who possessed only wealth or military force.

Eastern Jin power also followed the river. Jiankang and the lower Yangtze held the court and many émigré estates. Farther upstream, commanders such as Wang Dun (王敦 Wáng Dūn, 266–324), Tao Kan (陶侃 Táo Kǎn, 259–334) and Huan Wen (桓温 Huán Wēn, 312–373) controlled armies recruited from refugee communities. The court needed these forces to defend the south, but successful generals could force the emperor to submit or claim the throne. By the second generation, many northern families had settled comfortably in the south. Recovering the north could cost them their estates and create a conquering general powerful enough to end the Sima dynasty.

## Fei River and the survival of the south

By 383, [[id:catalog-R_SIXTEEN_FORMER_QIN|Former Qin]] (前秦 Qián Qín) under Fu Jian (苻坚 Fú Jiān, 338–385) had brought much of northern China under one ruler. Fu Jian then sent a large army south. Eastern Jin met it with the Northern Headquarters Troops (北府兵 Běifǔ Bīng), a trained force assembled under Xie Xuan (谢玄 Xiè Xuán) largely from commanders and fighters in militarized refugee settlements. At the [[id:fei-river|Battle of the Fei River]] (淝水之战 Féishuǐ zhī Zhàn), Jin forces attacked as the northern army tried to make room for a river crossing. The retreat became a rout. Traditional accounts give spectacular army totals and memorable boasts, but those figures and details remain uncertain.

The defeat exposed the weak loyalties inside Former Qin's rapidly assembled coalition. Commanders and regional leaders broke away, and the northern state fragmented over the following years. By 383, refugee communities supplied the southern court with a professional military base. Eastern Jin gained time rather than control of the north. Its victory allowed the court and its powerful families to survive, while the [[id:sixteen|Sixteen Kingdoms]] (十六国 Shíliù Guó) continued to compete beyond the Yangtze.

## Culture in an age of displacement

The collapse of the Western Jin court did not stop intellectual and artistic work. Writers associated with Mysterious Learning (玄学 Xuánxué) re-read Confucian and Daoist texts while debating personal conduct, government and withdrawal from public life. Pure conversation (清谈 qīngtán), poetry, music and elegant bearing became ways to judge character and rank members of the elite. Refusing office or retiring to a country estate could also enhance a family's reputation, creating a respected social life beyond the formal court.

[[id:catalog-E_BUD_SPREAD|Buddhist communities]] expanded, translated texts and built relationships with rulers and wealthy patrons in both north and south. Monasteries and Daoist groups brought people together through shared belief as well as kinship or service to a ruler. Foreign monks joined philosophical conversations at the Eastern Jin court, while southern Daoist traditions used revealed texts, poetry and calligraphy to attract established families.

Wang Xizhi (王羲之 Wáng Xīzhī, 303–361) became the model for running-script calligraphy; later copies preserve the celebrated preface associated with the [[id:orchid-pavilion|Orchid Pavilion]] (兰亭 Lántíng) gathering of 353. Gu Kaizhi (顾恺之 Gù Kǎizhī, about 344–406) was remembered as a master of figure painting, although works linked to him survive through later copies and attributions. [[id:tao-yuanming|Tao Yuanming]] (陶渊明 Táo Yuānmíng, 365–427) made withdrawal from office and farming life into enduring subjects of poetry. Their reputations were shaped by later transmission, but their work grew from a society in which culture could carry as much status as office, wealth or military command.

## China and Rome after western collapse

Jin China and the Roman world both faced civil war, migration and rival military coalitions across large territories. Western Jin lost its northern capitals in 311 and 316; the last western Roman emperor was removed in 476. In each case, a court claiming imperial continuity survived elsewhere: Eastern Jin in southern China and the Eastern Roman Empire at Constantinople.

The later paths differed. No Roman emperor restored durable rule over the entire former western empire. In China, competing courts continued to use a shared written political tradition and the claim that one emperor should rule all China. When the [[id:sui|Sui dynasty]] (隋朝 Suí Cháo) [[id:reunification|conquered the southern Chen in 589]], it joined a northern military and political centre to a southern region that had become far more populous, productive and culturally influential during the centuries of division.

## Note

The combined Jin chronology used here is 266–420: Western Jin, 266–316; Eastern Jin, 317–420. Some chronologies place the foundation in 265 because of differences in converting the traditional calendar. Sima Rui became king of Jin in 317 and emperor in 318. Traditional troop and casualty totals for the Yongjia disasters and Fei River are treated as literary claims, not precise modern statistics.
