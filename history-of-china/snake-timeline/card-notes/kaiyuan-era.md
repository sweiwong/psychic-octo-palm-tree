The Kaiyuan era (开元 Kāiyuán, 713–741) was the second reign period of Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (唐玄宗 Táng Xuánzōng, personal name Li Longji 李隆基 Lǐ Lóngjī), after the brief Xiantian era (先天 Xiāntiān, 712–713). Later writers remembered these decades as the “flourishing Kaiyuan age” (开元盛世 Kāiyuán shèngshì): a high point of [[tang|Tang]] government, wealth and culture. That reputation was real and influential, but official success did not reach every household equally.

## From palace conflict to settled rule

The [[tang|Tang dynasty]] (唐 Táng) had ruled since 618, inheriting a reunited empire and major institutions from the [[id:sui|Sui dynasty]] (隋 Suí). Its ruling family survived a turbulent succession after [[wu-zhou|Wu Zetian]] (武则天 Wǔ Zétiān) was removed in 705. Li Longji helped overthrow Empress Wei (韦皇后 Wéi Huánghòu) in 710 and restore his father, Emperor Ruizong (唐睿宗 Táng Ruìzōng). Ruizong abdicated in Li Longji’s favour in 712, but the new emperor secured full control only after defeating the faction of Princess Taiping (太平公主 Tàipíng Gōngzhǔ) in 713.

Xuanzong’s early chief ministers Yao Chong (姚崇 Yáo Chóng) and Song Jing (宋璟 Sòng Jǐng) became associated with careful appointments, lower court spending and more disciplined administration. Their reputations helped later historians present Kaiyuan as an example of a ruler listening to capable ministers. The emperor still made the final decisions, and effective government depended on officials far beyond the capital.

## Revenue, grain and the work of governing

Officials tried to make land and people visible to the tax system. From 721, Yuwen Rong (宇文融 Yǔwén Róng) led a registration drive known as kuohu (括户 kuòhù), seeking escaped or unregistered households (逃户 táohù) and land missing from official records. Transmitted histories credit the campaign with locating more than 800,000 households, while also recording complaints that local officials inflated totals and disturbed settled residents. A further large registration followed around 736. The exercise expanded revenue, but it also exposed how imperfectly a central government could count a mobile population.

In 740 the official register recorded about 8.41 million households and 48.14 million people. These were administrative totals, not a modern census of everyone living under Tang influence. Exempt people, unregistered migrants and communities outside directly governed prefectures make estimates of the actual population less certain.

The court maintained inherited ever-normal granaries (常平仓 chángpíngcāng) and relied on grain transport (漕运 cáoyùn) to provision the northern capitals. The [[id:sui-grand-canal|Grand Canal]] (大运河 Dà Yùnhé) linked productive regions around the Yangtze River to Luoyang (洛阳 Luòyáng) and the routes west toward [[chang-an|Chang’an]]. Storehouses, canals, boat crews and local tax collection made metropolitan abundance possible.

The familiar Kaiyuan tongbao coin (开元通宝 Kāiyuán tōngbǎo) can be misleading. It was introduced in 621 under Emperor Gaozu, almost a century before Xuanzong’s era, and its name meant an “inaugural currency,” not the Kaiyuan reign title. Its long circulation nevertheless shows the shared monetary system within which Kaiyuan taxes and trade operated.

## Tang and its neighbouring states

Kaiyuan power was extensive but uneven. Directly governed prefectures, military protectorates, allied rulers, tributary relationships and contested zones should not be coloured as if they were all identical provinces. In the west, the Protectorate General to Pacify the West (安西大都护府 Ānxī Dàdūhùfǔ) and Beiting Protectorate (北庭大都护府 Běitíng Dàdūhùfǔ) guarded routes through the Tarim Basin, but authority beyond the garrisons depended on local oasis rulers and shifting alliances.

The court fought and negotiated with the Tibetan Empire (吐蕃 Tǔbō), the Second Turkic Khaganate (后突厥汗国 Hòu Tūjué Hánguó) and the Türgesh (突骑施 Tūqíshī). Silla (新罗 Xīnluó) and Balhae (渤海 Bóhǎi) stood to the northeast, while Nanzhao (南诏 Nánzhào) emerged in the southwest in 738. Japanese embassies crossed the sea to study Tang government, religion and urban culture.

Farther west, Tang envoys and Central Asian rulers encountered the Umayyad Arabs, known broadly in Chinese records as Dashi (大食 Dàshí). The Abbasids, later called the “Black-robed Dashi” (黑衣大食 Hēiyī Dàshí), took power only in 750, after Kaiyuan. Their forces defeated a Tang-led army at the [[talas|Battle of Talas]] (怛罗斯之战 Dáluósī zhī Zhàn) in 751, but warfare did not end trade or diplomacy between the two worlds.

## A Silk Roads crossroads

The Silk Roads were a network of land and sea routes, not one road owned by one empire. The earlier [[id:catalog-E_HAN_2|mission of Zhang Qian]] (张骞 Zhāng Qiān) had become a famous point in the history of these connections, but eighth-century exchange depended on generations of merchants, translators, guides, sailors and oasis communities.

Sogdia (粟特 Sùtè) was a region of city-states centred on places including Samarkand, known in Tang records as Kang (康国 Kāngguó), rather than a single neighbouring empire. Sogdians (粟特人 Sùtèrén) were prominent in overland commerce and also worked in China as officials, soldiers, translators, religious specialists and entertainers. Silk, ceramics and paper moved west; horses, glass, metalwork, textiles, grapes and musical forms moved east. Objects and techniques often passed through several hands, so “Chinese” and “foreign” goods could be remade along the route.

[[id:mogao-caves|Mogao Caves at Dunhuang]] (敦煌莫高窟 Dūnhuáng Mògāo Kū) preserve another side of this connected world. Donors, artists and religious communities created images and manuscripts there over centuries. The caves show that Silk Roads exchange carried beliefs, stories and artistic conventions as well as merchandise.

## A cosmopolitan capital

[[chang-an|Chang’an]] (长安 Cháng’ān) was a planned imperial capital with 108 residential wards and two separately walled official markets. Its population cannot be counted precisely, but it was among the largest cities of its time. The markets and court drew people and goods from across East and Central Asia without making all residents equal before the law or equally welcome.

Sogdian and Persian residents, Central Asian musicians, Buddhist monks, Japanese and Korean students, diplomats, soldiers and craftspeople all contributed to the city’s life. Zoroastrianism (祆教 Xiānjiào), Manichaeism (摩尼教 Móníjiào), Buddhism (佛教 Fójiào) and East Syriac Christianity, later called Jingjiao (景教 Jǐngjiào), had communities or institutions in Tang China. Central Asian music (胡乐 húyuè), the pear-shaped pipa (琵琶 pípá) and dances such as the “Sogdian whirl” (胡旋舞 Húxuánwǔ) entered elite taste. Cosmopolitanism was not only a court fashion: it was made by migrants and resident communities who carried skills, languages and family networks with them.

## Poetry, pilgrimage and memory

Kaiyuan overlapped with the lives of [[li-bai-du-fu|Li Bai (李白 Lǐ Bái) and Du Fu (杜甫 Dù Fǔ)]], although much of Du Fu’s mature work responded to the upheavals that followed. Their later fame helped turn the High Tang into a literary ideal. Court patronage mattered, but poems also circulated through friendships, travel, performance and the search for office.

Buddhist monasteries, translators and pilgrims connected the court to wider Asian traditions. [[xuanzang-return|Xuanzang]] (玄奘 Xuánzàng) had returned from India in 645, decades before Kaiyuan, yet the texts translated by his teams remained central to religious study. The prosperity of institutions is easier to see in surviving temples, sculptures and manuscripts than the labour and donations that sustained them.

## From Kaiyuan to Tianbao

Xuanzong changed the reign title to Tianbao (天宝 Tiānbǎo) in 742. Li Linfu (李林甫 Lǐ Línfǔ) dominated the government for much of the following decade, and Yang Yuhuan received the title Yang Guifei (杨贵妃 Yáng Guìfēi) in 745. These belong to the Tianbao period, not Kaiyuan itself.

Frontier armies increasingly relied on long-service professionals under [[jiedushi|military commissioners]] (节度使 jiédùshǐ). This could improve defence while concentrating soldiers, supplies and patronage in regional commands. In 755, the commissioner An Lushan (安禄山 Ān Lùshān) rebelled, beginning the [[an-lushan|An–Shi Rebellion]] (安史之乱 Ān-Shǐ zhī luàn). The disaster changed Tang government permanently, but it should not be read backward as an outcome already fixed in 741. Kaiyuan’s achievements and the later crisis grew from changing institutions, choices and conflicts rather than a simple moral cycle of luxury and decline.

## Note

713–741 is the Kaiyuan era, not Xuanzong’s entire reign. Official household figures are administrative records rather than a count of the whole population. Kaiyuan tongbao predates the reign title, Baghdad was not founded until 762, and the Abbasid Caliphate began after Kaiyuan.
