The Three Kingdoms is the sixty-year division of China between [[id:catalog-R_WEI|Wei]] (曹魏 Cáo Wèi), [[id:catalog-R_SHU|Shu-Han]] (蜀汉 Shǔ-Hàn) and [[id:catalog-R_WU|Wu]] (孙吴 Sūn Wú), dated from the [[id:catalog-E_3K_1|abdication of the last Han emperor in 220]] to the surrender of Wu in 280. It is the most heavily mythologised period in Chinese history and among the most thinly documented. Almost everything known about it comes from a record compiled under the dynasty that absorbed the winner, and almost everything popularly believed about it comes from a novel finished a thousand years later.

## From the Han collapse to three courts

The formal division of 220 followed more than thirty years of civil war that began with the [[id:yellow-turbans|Yellow Turban uprising]] of 184. Rafe de Crespigny dates the effective end of [[id:catalog-R_HAN_E|Eastern Han]] (东汉 Dōng Hàn) rule to 189, when the general Dong Zhuo (董卓 Dǒng Zhuó, d. 192) seized the court in a burning Luoyang (洛阳 Luòyáng) and the boy he enthroned, Emperor Xian (汉献帝 Hàn Xiàndì, r. 189–220), began thirty-one years in the custody of one warlord after another. Cao Cao (曹操 Cáo Cāo, 155–220) took the emperor into his own keeping in 196 and governed as a loyal [[id:han|Han]] minister while building a parallel administration. Victory at Guandu (官渡 Guāndù) in 200 opened the north, and by 207 he held the whole North China plain. Defeat at [[id:catalog-E_3K_2|Red Cliffs]] (赤壁 Chìbì) in the winter of 208 cost him the Yangtze and the south; he claimed 800,000 men in that campaign, and the commander who beat him put the real figure at a quarter of that.

Cao Pi (曹丕 Cáo Pī, 187–226) took Emperor Xian's ceded abdication (禅让 shànràng) in December 220 and ruled Wei from Luoyang. Liu Bei (刘备 Liú Bèi, 161–223) declared himself emperor at Chengdu (成都 Chéngdū) in 221 on a claimed descent from the [[id:catalog-R_HAN_W|Former Han house]] and called his own state Han. Sun Quan (孙权 Sūn Quán, 182–252) waited longest: a kingship from Wei in 221, his own reign title in 222, the imperial title only in 229, on the argument that the Han succession had simply lapsed and no worthy heir had claimed it. No traditional historian has accepted his case.

## What Wei built

Wei held roughly two-thirds of the empire's registered population along with the old capitals and the central plains, and its strength rested on machinery Cao Cao had assembled during the wars. Agricultural garrisons (屯田 túntián), begun around 196 on land abandoned by refugees, gave tenants state land and tools for half the harvest, more where the plough oxen were the state's too, and answered to the central government rather than the commandery they sat in. Soldiers came from hereditary military households (士家 shìjiā) whose sons inherited the obligation. Officials entered through the nine-rank system (九品中正制 jiǔpǐn zhōngzhèngzhì), credited to Chen Qun (陈群 Chén Qún, d. 236) under Cao Pi in 220 though it had been taking shape under Cao Cao for years, in which Rectifiers graded candidates in their home commandery into nine categories. It was meant to bring the great families under central control and did the reverse: a memorial of the [[id:catalog-R_JIN_W|Western Jin]] (西晋 Xī Jìn) complained that no man of humble birth reached the high grades and no member of a powerful clan the low ones.

## Shu and Wu

Shu-Han was a warlord regime in one province, Yi (益州 Yìzhōu), behind the mountain passes of the upper Yangtze. Zhuge Liang (诸葛亮 Zhūgě Liàng, 181–234) governed as regent from Liu Bei's death in 223, settled the southwest, then spent his last seven years on [[id:zhuge-northern-expeditions|five expeditions north]], which took one frontier commandery and never broke the Wei line. Government at Chengdu decayed after him under Liu Shan (刘禅 Liú Shàn, 207–271) and his favourites, and the state never appointed historians at all.

Wu was a coalition of southern magnates who kept their own private armies and accepted the Sun family above them, and it expanded into its own hinterland. Campaigns against the Shanyue (山越 Shānyuè) hill peoples of the southeast turned them into registered taxpayers and conscripts who financed the next advance, and Wu's authority reached the Red River delta. Counties in Wu's territory roughly doubled between the Later Han census and the Jin register, from 160 to 322; in Shu's territory the gain was about a fifth. This is the achievement of the period that lasted longest.

## The staggered end

In 249 Sima Yi (司马懿 Sīmǎ Yì, 179–251) seized the capital in a coup and destroyed the ruling regent and his kinsmen. Wei was in Sima hands from then on. The three states did not fall together. Shu went first, in 263, when a Wei army crossed the mountain passes and Liu Shan surrendered after one pitched battle. Wei ended next, when Sima Yan (司马炎 Sīmǎ Yán, 236–290) received the last Cao emperor's abdication in February 266 in a ceremony copied from Cao Pi's, and the [[id:jin-early|Jin]] (晋 Jìn) took the throne without a war. Wu lasted another fourteen years, until a fleet built upriver in Yi province came down through the Yangtze gorges and Sun Hao (孙皓 Sūn Hào, r. 264–280) surrendered in 280. The Jin registers of about 280 count some 16.2 million people against 56.5 million in the Han census of 157, a fall that measures the reach of tax administration as much as mortality, since Hans Bielenstein showed the Jin figures to be tax quotas rather than counts of persons. The wars killed on an enormous scale even so.

## A record written by the winners' heirs

The Records of the Three Kingdoms (三国志 Sānguózhì), compiled by Chen Shou (陈寿 Chén Shòu, 233–297), is the only near-contemporary history of the period, and it is written entirely as biography, so almost everything institutional has to be reconstructed from elsewhere. Chen Shou was a man of Shu who served its court and then wrote as an official of Jin, the dynasty that had taken Wei's throne; his book gives Wei's rulers imperial annals and Shu's and Wu's the rank of lords. Wu's official history survives only where Pei Songzhi (裴松之 Péi Sōngzhī, 372–451) quoted it in the annotation he completed in 429. One of his sources is the Biography of Cao the Trickster (曹瞒传 Cáo Mán zhuàn), a hostile Wu account whose stories of Cao Cao's cruelty and play-acting appear nowhere else and pass directly into the villain of later drama. Xiaofei Tian has traced how the loss compounded: the sixth-century Wen xuan (文选 Wén xuǎn), compiled while Wu literary collections still circulated, chose exactly two pieces by Shu or Wu authors.

## Why the Three Kingdoms matters

Chen Shou's judgement that Wei was the Han's heir, on the strength of its power and its hold on the central plains, was ordinary opinion in his lifetime. Xi Zuochi (习凿齿 Xí Záochǐ, d. 384) attacked the view under the Eastern Jin: Wei had usurped the throne and failed to reunify the empire, so its succession counted for nothing, which raised Shu-Han, whose founder was a Liu. The question of legitimate succession (正统 zhèngtǒng) gained little ground while China was united. It revived under the Song, and [[id:song-zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]] (朱熹 Zhū Xī, 1130–1200), born three years after the Song lost the north, made Shu the legitimate line: what a ruler does settles the question, and where he does it does not. Who counted as legitimate in the third century tracked the anxieties of the century doing the judging.

Popular sympathy had gone that way long before the scholars did. Su Shi (苏轼 Sū Shì, 1037–1101) recorded that children sent to hear the street storytellers of the [[id:catalog-R_NSONG|Northern Song]] wept when Liu Bei was defeated and cheered when Cao Cao was. Out of that tradition came [[id:yuan|Yuan]] drama and eventually the Romance of the Three Kingdoms (三国演义 Sānguó yǎnyì), attributed to Luo Guanzhong (罗贯中 Luó Guànzhōng) and fixed in its standard form only by a recension of the 1660s. The novel reverses Chen Shou: Cao Cao is the usurper, Zhuge Liang summons the wind at Red Cliffs where he in fact held no command, and Guan Yu (关羽 Guān Yǔ, d. 219), a middling general executed in a failed campaign, becomes a god whom [[id:ming|Ming]] and [[id:qing|Qing]] emperors raised to imperial rank. De Crespigny's verdict is that the novel is worth as much for third-century history as Shakespeare's plays are for the history of England, and Xiaofei Tian argues that the period was built in retrospect from the start.

The reunification of 280 held for about a decade. Sima Yan died in 290, the [[id:eight-princes|War of the Eight Princes]] began in 291, and the north was [[id:sixteen|lost in the 310s]]. From 317 the [[id:catalog-R_JIN_EASTERN_JIN|Jin court]] ruled from Jiankang (建康 Jiànkāng), the city Wu had founded as Jianye (建业 Jiànyè) and now Nanjing, on ground that sixty years of Wu administration had made taxable. The Three Kingdoms opened the [[id:division|division of China]] that ran to 589, and its two real bequests are the aristocratic grip that the nine ranks made hereditary and that outlasted the [[id:sui-examinations|Sui examinations]] by three centuries, and a governed [[id:southward-economic-shift|south]].
