The Sui dynasty lasted from 581 to 618. In 37 years, it reunited an empire divided for more than three centuries, joined north and south through a vast canal network, and imposed legal and administrative reforms that later dynasties kept reshaping.

After the [Han Dynasty](../index.html?card=han) collapsed in 220, the Sui rose from a northern military-aristocratic family. Its founder, Emperor Wen of Sui, took the Northern Zhou throne in 581 and conquered Chen in 589, [reuniting the principal northern and southern states](../index.html?card=reunification). Most of the dynasty fell under two reigns, Wen and his son Emperor Yang. The [Tang Dynasty](../index.html?card=tang) inherited the Sui state in 618.

## Geopolitical context

The Western Roman Empire had fallen more than a century earlier. The Byzantine Empire remained a power in the eastern Mediterranean, while the Sasanian Persian Empire controlled routes between the Mediterranean and Central and South Asia. China had been divided among competing northern and southern governments since the early fourth century, during the [Northern and Southern Dynasties](../index.html?card=north-south). Northern courts drew on both farming and steppe traditions and included rulers from several ethnic backgrounds. Southern courts governed from the Yangtze valley and developed their own political and cultural institutions.

The conquest of Chen in 589 ended this division at the level of imperial rule. For the first time since the [Western Jin](../index.html?card=catalog-R_JIN_W), one court claimed authority from the northern frontier to the southern coast. Conquest changed the political map quickly. Making that territory governable took longer.

## The two emperors

Emperor Wen of Sui, who ruled from 581 to 604, lowered taxes, revised harsh laws, resettled farmland, and supported public granaries that stored grain for shortages. Household registers compiled under the Sui counted about nine million households and roughly 46 million people. These records measured the state’s registered tax base, so they were never a modern census and did not capture everyone.

Emperor Yang, who ruled from 604 to 618, expanded the scale and speed of state projects. He built a new eastern capital at Luoyang, extended the Grand Canal network, repaired sections of the northern frontier walls, and launched three major campaigns against Goguryeo.

## The Grand Canal

The [Grand Canal](../index.html?card=sui-grand-canal) was a network of natural rivers, lakes, older channels, and new excavations. Sui work linked Luoyang to the lower Yangtze and extended another branch toward the northern frontier. The route changed under later dynasties, so the modern Beijing to Hangzhou line should not be projected unchanged onto the Sui system.

The canal carried tax grain from the productive Yangtze valley toward northern capitals and frontier armies. Northern political and military centres could not reliably feed themselves from nearby farmland alone. Water transport moved bulk grain more efficiently than carts and pack animals.

Contemporary chronicles claim that millions of labourers were conscripted for the canals, palaces, roads, and frontier works. The exact totals are disputed, but the demands were immense. The canal bound distant regions into one tax and supply system while placing its immediate cost on the people ordered to dig and maintain it. Parts of the Grand Canal still carry freight, and UNESCO calls the whole system the world’s longest and oldest canal.

## Law and government

In 583, Emperor Wen issued the Kaihuang Code. It reduced the number and severity of punishments, standardized sentencing, and strengthened the central government’s control over law. The code helped shape the later [Tang legal code](../index.html?card=tang-code), which influenced legal systems in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.

The Sui also helped establish the [civil service examination system](../index.html?card=sui-examinations). Early examinations selected only a small share of officials. Family standing, recommendations, and aristocratic connections still mattered. Written tests became more important under later dynasties and reached a much larger scale under the [Song Dynasty](../index.html?card=song).

## Military overreach and collapse

Between 612 and 614, Emperor Yang launched three invasions of Goguryeo, a kingdom that controlled northern Korea and territory in southern Manchuria. Sui records assign more than one million troops and support personnel to the first campaign. The figure may be inflated, but the expedition was enormous by pre-modern standards.

The first invasion ended in disaster. At the Salsu River, Goguryeo forces destroyed much of a retreating Sui army. Traditional accounts claim that fewer than 3,000 men returned from a force of more than 300,000; those numbers also demand caution. The failed wars drained the treasury, disrupted farming through conscription, and weakened the ruling house.

Rebellions spread from 613. By 617, the Sui court had lost control of most of the empire. Emperor Yang was killed by his own officers at Jiangdu in April 618. Emperor Gong, whom a rival faction had placed on the throne, was deposed in June as Li Yuan founded the Tang dynasty.

## What the Tang inherited

The Tang inherited a recently reunited territory, the canal network, legal reforms, government offices, granaries, and the beginnings of examination recruitment. It retained useful parts of the Sui state and changed them over time. The infrastructure outlived the court that had forced people to build it.

The Sui invites comparison with the [Qin Dynasty](../index.html?card=qin). Both reunited divided territories, standardized government, demanded heavy labour for large projects, and fell within a few decades. Each was followed by a longer-lived dynasty that kept parts of its predecessor’s system.

## Cultural production

Emperor Wen promoted Buddhism across a territory where religious practice had developed differently under northern and southern courts. Monasteries, relic distributions, translations, sculpture, and ritual helped the new dynasty present one sacred order across the reunited empire. Sui Buddhist sculpture stands between the leaner forms associated with Northern Wei and the fuller figures of the Tang. The [Longmen Grottoes](../index.html?card=longmen-caves) preserve part of that longer history, although the Sui-period sculpture shown here has no documented Longmen provenance.

Sui ceramics also brought northern lead-glazed wares and southern celadon traditions into closer contact. The Zhaozhou Bridge, also called the Anji Bridge, was completed in Hebei in 605. Designed by Li Chun, it is the world’s oldest surviving open-spandrel stone arch bridge. Its main span measures about 37 metres, and the bridge remains in use.

## Five dates

In 581, Yang Jian took the throne and founded the Sui dynasty. In 589, Sui armies defeated Chen and reunited north and south. In 605, work began on the eastern capital at Luoyang and the Tongji Canal. In 612, Emperor Yang launched the first great invasion of Goguryeo, which ended in disaster at the Salsu River. In 618, Emperor Yang was killed at Jiangdu and Emperor Gong was deposed as the Tang dynasty began.

## Note

581–618 is the conventional dynastic span. Emperor Wen and Emperor Yang dominated the period, while Emperor Gong formally reigned from 617 to 618. Major canal construction continued after 605, including work on the Yongji Canal in 608 and the Jiangnan Canal in 610.
