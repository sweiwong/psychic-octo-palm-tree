Xuanzang returned to Chang’an in 645 carrying Buddhist manuscripts. He had begun his journey despite an imperial travel prohibition. The traveller who left without permission now received recognition at the imperial centre.

## The work of translation

Bringing manuscripts home began another long task. Translators needed to compare texts, decide on meanings and work together with continuing institutional support. Xuanzang sought to resolve differences among the Buddhist writings already available in China. His account of the western regions also preserved knowledge of societies beyond [[tang|Tang rule]].

## Learning from beyond the empire

Xuanzang studied Buddhist thought at Nalanda (那烂陀寺), a major monastery and centre of learning in India, before bringing texts back to China. Buddhism was already established in China before Xuanzang travelled. He helped transform that existing tradition by bringing new texts and expertise into it. Chang’an’s intellectual life depended partly on knowledge acquired elsewhere and made accessible through collective work. Later religious biographies emphasized his heroic journey, while the translation enterprise required many people after he arrived home.

## Note

Return: 645 CE. Accounts differ on his departure year; this marker does not depend on choosing between them.
