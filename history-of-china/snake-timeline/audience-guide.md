# Writing for curious Mandarin learners

This is Wei's passion project: an illustrated guide to Chinese history for curious beginners. The first intended community is Mandarin Blueprint's SKOOL group, with 3,717 members as reported by Wei in September 2026. Readers include foreign learners and overseas-born Chinese people exploring a language and history they may not have grown up knowing.

## What a reading card should do

Give readers their bearings, explain something worth knowing, and leave them curious to explore another subject. Assume intelligence and curiosity, but no prior knowledge of Chinese history. Explain unfamiliar historical terms in ordinary English. Keep English, Simplified Chinese, and accurate pinyin together.

Use a familiar, obvious title for the person, event, period, or object. Avoid obscure teasers such as “A dated offering changes ceramic history.” The title should help readers find a subject and recognise it again. Keep qualifiers that distinguish uncertain dates, legendary people, or different states with similar names.

Open with the people and the situation. Explain how something worked, what choices were available, and what followed. Use specific details already supported by the research. Two short sections can develop the story without a long introduction. Do not force every card into identical headings, rhetorical questions, or “why it matters” conclusions.

The desired register is an accessible history documentary or conversational history programme. This is a direction for clarity and pacing, not permission to copy a presenter's wording or invent dialogue, scenery, motives, or certainty.

## Evidence and limits

Explain the history directly. Keep academic names and bibliographic details in the linked sources unless identifying an author is necessary to understand a disagreement. Distinguish facts, interpretation, uncertainty, and later legends in clear English. Never turn a contested claim into an unqualified fact merely to simplify a sentence.

Retain all audited dates, date labels, source links, image captions, credits, and reuse terms. Pictures should help readers understand a person, place, object, or event. Identify later depictions and contextual images honestly. Do not imply that drama costumes or fictional dynasties are historical evidence.

Examples approved by Wei: “Ming Voyages,” “End of Imperial China,” and “Second World War: War with Japan.” Their cards still explain that 1405 marks the first voyage, the Republic was founded before the Qing abdication, and China's full-scale war with Japan began in 1937.

## Editorial files

`beginner_early.js`, `beginner_middle.js`, and `beginner_late.js` hold reader-facing copy. `beginner_edition.js` applies it after the research packs. The original research stays available for provenance. Only names, introductions, section text, and readable notes can change in this layer; historical fields are protected by checks.

Wei approved removing `taiwan-democratization` from the reading edition. Its research remains archived in the source pack and date audit. The separate ROC in Taiwan card remains. The active reading edition has 205 cards and retains all 111 original workbook subjects.
