# Song dynasty · 宋 · Sòng

**960–1279**

The Song (宋 Sòng) dynasty ruled much of China from 960 to 1279. Its era brought government-issued paper money, movable-type printing and celebrated landscape painting. Founded by Emperor Taizu (太祖 Tàizǔ), it reunited much of a divided China before losing the north in 1127; its southern court survived until the Mongol conquest.

## Taizu founds the dynasty

In 960, an army commander seized the throne from a child emperor. His name was Zhao Kuangyin (赵匡胤 Zhào Kuāngyìn), better known as Emperor Taizu (r. 960–976). Backed by his troops, he forced the ruler of Later Zhou (后周 Hòu Zhōu) to abdicate and founded the Song.

China had been divided since the fall of the [[tang|Tang]] (唐 Táng). In the north, military leaders repeatedly overthrew one another; in the south, rival kingdoms ruled independently. Later Zhou was the last of the northern regimes in this [[five-dynasties|Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period]] (五代十国 Wǔdài Shíguó). When its emperor died in 959, his young son inherited the throne. Taizu took power the following year.

From his capital at Kaifeng (开封 Kāifēng), Taizu conquered several southern kingdoms and brought their tax revenues under Song control. His brother and successor, Emperor Taizong (太宗 Tàizōng, r. 976–997), completed the remaining conquests and submissions by 979. Reuniting much of China took the two emperors nearly twenty years.

Taizu knew that another general could overthrow him in turn. He and his successors put civilian officials in charge of regional government, brought strong military units into the central army and rotated commanders. Generals had less freedom to build the personal armies that had brought earlier rulers to power.

## Northern Song and Southern Song

The Northern Song (北宋 Běi Sòng, 960–1127) ruled from Kaifeng over much of northern and southern China. It shared the wider region with powerful neighbours: the Khitan-ruled [[liao|Liao]] (辽 Liáo) to the north and the Tangut-ruled [[western-xia|Western Xia]] (西夏 Xī Xià) to the northwest. Song campaigns failed to recover all the northern lands its rulers claimed. Agreements with [[chanyuan|Liao in 1005]] and Xia in 1044 brought peace in return for annual payments.

Hoping to regain northern territory, Song joined the Jurchen-ruled [[jurchen-jin|Jin]] (金 Jīn) in an attack on Liao. Jin then turned against Song. Its armies [[jingkang|captured Kaifeng in 1127]] and took both the reigning emperor and his father prisoner.

Another member of the imperial family took the throne as Emperor Gaozong (高宗 Gāozōng, r. 1127–1162). His court fled south and, after years on the move, settled at Hangzhou (杭州 Hángzhōu). Officially called Lin'an (临安 Lín'ān), it became the capital in 1138.

The Southern Song (南宋 Nán Sòng, 1127–1279) was the same dynasty ruling from a new capital. It had lost the north, but retained populous southern lands with productive farms and busy markets. Their wealth sustained the court and its armies for another century and a half. A peace agreement with Jin in 1141–42 recognised the division, although Song rulers continued to hope for recovery of the north.

## Governing through educated officials

Song emperors relied increasingly on officials selected through the civil examinations (科举 kējǔ). Candidates studied classical texts and practised the writing required for government service. Passing could win a government post and bring status to a whole family. Parents invested in schooling and books to give their sons that chance.

Years of study were expensive. Families already connected to government could afford better preparation and had other routes into office. Women could study and teach their children, but could not sit the examinations or enter the civil service.

Civilian officials also supervised a large professional army. Paying soldiers and defending the borders put heavy demands on the treasury. From 1069, [[song-wang-anshi|Wang Anshi]] (王安石 Wáng Ānshí), supported by Emperor Shenzong (神宗 Shénzōng), introduced reforms that included government loans to farmers, changes to labour obligations and local militia training. He hoped to help farming households and increase government revenue. Opponents disputed how the reforms worked and whom they helped. The struggle over his policies outlasted his years in office.

## Rice, paper money and sea trade

New rice varieties helped feed the growing towns. Early-ripening, drought-resistant Champa rice from Southeast Asia could grow in places less suited to older varieties. Farmers also extended irrigation and brought more land into cultivation. More food supported a larger population, while farming families sold surplus crops and specialised in products such as tea and silk. The [[southward-economic-shift|south's economic expansion]] was already well advanced before the court moved there in 1127.

Song merchants and officials developed new ways to pay for this trade. In Sichuan (四川 Sìchuān), merchants used paper notes to avoid carrying heavy iron coins. In 1023, the government set up an office to issue [[song-paper-money|paper currency]]. Paper notes could carry a large value without the weight of metal, although coins remained in use.

Large ships carried Chinese ceramics, silk and books overseas. Magnetic compasses helped sailors navigate, and watertight compartments limited the spread of water through a damaged hull. Taxes on maritime commerce supplied government revenue. Southern Song also maintained a powerful navy, which fought for a state increasingly dependent on its rivers and coast.

## Movable type and gunpowder weapons

Bi Sheng (毕昇 Bì Shēng) developed movable type in the 1040s. Printers could arrange individual clay characters to make a page, then reuse them for another text. Woodblock printing, in which a whole page was carved on a block, remained more widely used. The larger expansion of printing put examination guides, religious works and practical manuals within reach of more readers.

Gunpowder acquired new military uses during the Song. Armies used [[song-gunpowder|gunpowder incendiary weapons]], including grenades thrown by catapults, and military manuals recorded recipes for making the powder. These weapons spread fire during sieges. Song's enemies also acquired its technology, so inventions could give an advantage without guaranteeing victory.

## Landscape painting and Confucian learning

Song painters made mountains, rivers and forests the main subjects of major works. In a monumental landscape by Fan Kuan (范宽 Fàn Kuān), towering peaks dwarf the travellers below. Ink and brushwork could suggest rock, mist and distance on a hanging scroll. Landscape painting became one of the best-known achievements of Chinese art.

Potters produced ceramics for a wide market. Cizhou (磁州 Cízhōu) was known for black-and-white stoneware, including vases decorated with carved peonies. Similar wares were made elsewhere in northern China. Merchants carried bowls, dishes and vases from regional workshops to buyers across China and overseas.

Confucian scholars reconsidered how people should learn and live. [[song-zhu-xi|Zhu Xi]] (朱熹 Zhū Xī) helped establish academies and taught that study should shape personal conduct and family responsibilities. His commentaries on the Four Books (四书 Sìshū), a collection of Confucian texts, brought earlier teachings into an influential programme of moral education. His ideas faced opposition in his lifetime before gaining official support.

## Mongol conquest and what continued

The Mongols conquered Southern Song after decades of war. Chinese soldiers, sailors and engineers helped their armies besiege cities and fight on the rivers. The fall of the stronghold of Xiangyang (襄阳 Xiāngyáng) in 1273 opened an invasion route into the south. Hangzhou surrendered in 1276, but surviving members of the imperial family continued resistance. Their final naval defeat in 1279 ended Song rule.

Southern China became part of the Mongol [[yuan|Yuan]] (元 Yuán) dynasty. Song books remained in use, and later rulers adopted Zhu Xi's interpretations of the Confucian classics for the civil examinations. Students continued to study his work long after the dynasty that produced it had fallen.
