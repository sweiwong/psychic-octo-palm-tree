## The argument

The rebellion is normally told as the shock that broke a golden age. The better-supported claim is that it exposed a militarisation Xuanzong's own government had already built and then made it permanent. The militia system had stopped functioning before 755; the frontier was held by long-service professional armies under a handful of standing commands, of which An Lushan controlled three totalling roughly 184,000 men out of about 487,000 frontier troops. What the war changed was not the existence of provincial armies but their distribution: the arrangement spread inward from the border to cover the whole empire, in forty-odd provinces, several of them handed straight back to the men who had fought for the rebels. A second argument runs alongside and should qualify the first: the rebellion was read at the time, and has been read since, as a lesson about trusting non-Chinese generals, and that reading is itself a historical artefact. Sogdians fought on both sides in large numbers, Sogdian families in China changed their surnames afterwards to escape the association, and Shao-yun Yang has shown that the later "barbarised Hebei" framing was rhetoric aimed at political disobedience rather than a description of ethnic change. Finally, the numbers everyone quotes belong in the argument, not in a footnote: the registered population figures are a record of what the Tang state could still see, and Du You, the Tang official who compiled them, said as much himself.

## Facts

### The frontier command system before 755

- The office of military commissioner (节度使 jiédùshǐ) was created under Emperor Ruizong (睿宗 Ruìzōng, r. 684, 710–712) for border defence, and expanded under Xuanzong. By the Tianbao era there were ten standing commands, known as the ten commissioners of Tianbao (天宝十节度使). *Ulrich Theobald / ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- Troop strengths of the ten commands, from Jiu Tangshu 38 (Dili 1): Fanyang 91,400; Pinglu 37,500; Hedong 55,000; Shuofang 64,700; Hexi 73,000; Longyou 75,000; Anxi 24,000; Beiting 20,000; Jiannan 30,900; the five Lingnan prefectures 15,400. Total 486,900. *ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen.* An alternative tally of the same establishment gives about 489,400. *Wikipedia, Emperor Xuanzong.* Total imperial strength in 742 is given as 574,000, of which 490,000 were on the frontiers. *ChinaKnowledge, An Lushan Rebellion, citing Yang Zhijiu 1992*
- **An Lushan's three commands together held about 183,900 men, roughly 38 per cent of the frontier establishment.** (Arithmetic on the Jiu Tangshu table; present as approximate.)
- Xuanzong let the metropolitan guard forces around Chang'an decay while the frontier armies grew. *ChinaKnowledge, An Lushan Rebellion*
- David Graff puts the military participation ratio in 742 at roughly 1 soldier per 103 people, comparable to the late Roman empire and lighter than several early modern European states. *Graff, "The Reach of the Military: Tang"*
- The rotating militia system (府兵制 fǔbīngzhì) was abolished in 749, six years before the rebellion; the frontier had already shifted to long-service professional soldiers whose loyalty ran to their commanders. Defence spending rose from about 2 million strings of cash in 712 to 12 million in 742 and 15 million by 755. *Wikipedia, An Lushan rebellion (figures widely repeated from Twitchett's fiscal work; flag as approximate)*
- From about 747 the chancellor Li Linfu made a policy of filling frontier commands with generals of non-Chinese origin, on the reasoning that men without a literati background could not use a military reputation to move into the chancellorship and threaten him. *Encyclopaedia Iranica, "An Lu-shan"; EBSCO Research Starters; ChinaKnowledge*

### An Lushan: origins and rise

- Born about 703, most precisely dated by Pulleyblank to 10 February 703. *Pulleyblank, The Background of the Rebellion of An Lu-shan (1955), summarised in reviews and reference literature*
- Father Sogdian, mother a Turkic woman of the Ashide (阿史德) clan; his stepfather was An Yanyan (安延偃), from whom he took the surname An. *Pulleyblank; Wikipedia, An Lushan; EBSCO Research Starters*
- The surname An 安 is the standard Chinese surname for people from Bukhara, one of the "nine surnames of Zhaowu" (昭武九姓) used for Sogdians in China. Kang 康 marks Samarkand, Shi 史 marks Kesh (Shi Siming's surname), Shi 石 marks Chach/Tashkent, He 何 marks Kushaniyya. Of roughly 10,500 individuals named in excavated Dunhuang and Turfan documents, over 850 bear Zhaowu surnames. *Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Personal Names, Sogdian i. In Chinese Sources"*
- His given name is generally reconstructed as the Sogdian *Rōxšan*, "bright", the same element as in Roxana, transcribed into Chinese as 轧荦山 / 阿荦山. *Pulleyblank; James Carter, The China Project*
- He spoke six non-Chinese languages and worked as a broker and interpreter (互市牙郎 hùshì yáláng) in the frontier horse-and-silk markets before entering the army. *Wikipedia, An Lushan, citing the Tang biographies*
- Career: 741 commander at Yingzhou and in the Pinglu army; 742 military commissioner of Pinglu; 744 also Fanyang; 751 also Hedong, and created Prince of Dongping (东平郡王). *Wikipedia, An Lushan; ChinaKnowledge*
- Yingzhou (modern Chaoyang, Liaoning), the Pinglu seat where he grew up, held a Sogdian merchant community. A Tang tomb excavated near Chaoyang in 1975 produced a group of Sogdian silver vessels. Chinese sources record An Lushan presenting large quantities of gold and silver ware to Xuanzong before the rebellion. *Rong Xinjiang, "New Light on Sogdian Colonies along the Silk Road"*
- Rong Xinjiang identifies the Sogdian settlement with the greatest influence on Tang politics as the Six Hu Prefectures (六胡州) south of Lingzhou and Xiazhou, first studied by Pulleyblank in "A Sogdian Colony in Inner Mongolia", T'oung Pao 41 (1952). *Rong Xinjiang, same*
- In 751 Yang Guifei formally adopted him as her son. Court gossip about the relationship circulated at the time. *EBSCO Research Starters; ChinaKnowledge*
- Also in 751 he was heavily defeated by the Khitan, reportedly hampered by rain, and kept his commands regardless. *Wikipedia, An Lushan*
- Li Linfu died in the winter of 752–753 and Yang Guozhong, Yang Guifei's cousin, took the chancellorship. Yang Guozhong pressed Xuanzong repeatedly that An Lushan was preparing to rebel. *ChinaKnowledge; EBSCO*
- Late in life An Lushan was obese, going blind, and suffering from ulcers. *Wikipedia, An Lushan*

### The war, 755–763

- **16 December 755**: An Lushan marched out of Fanyang, claiming a secret imperial order to remove Yang Guozhong. Force given as about 164,000 (other tallies 150,000; he claimed 200,000). *Wikipedia, An Lushan rebellion; EBSCO gives 160,000*
- His eldest son An Qingzong (安庆宗), resident at Chang'an, was executed by the court when the news arrived.
- Northeastern prefects and magistrates fled, surrendered or were captured with little fighting. A scratch army of 60,000 under Feng Changqing was beaten outside Luoyang. *ChinaKnowledge*
- **18 January 756**: Luoyang fell, 33 days after the march began. *Wikipedia; EBSCO*
- **5 February 756**: An Lushan proclaimed himself emperor of Great Yan (大燕), era name Shengwu (圣武). *Wikipedia, Yan (An–Shi)*
- Feng Changqing and Gao Xianzhi withdrew to Tong Pass and were executed there for treason on accusations carried by the eunuch Bian Lingcheng (边令诚). The ailing Geshu Han was ordered to hold the pass. *ChinaKnowledge; Wikipedia, Feng Changqing*
- Resistance formed in Hebei behind Yan Zhenqing, prefect of Pingyuan, and his cousin Yan Gaoqing, prefect of Changshan, supported by Shuofang troops under Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi. It went well enough that An Lushan considered pulling back to the northeast. Changshan fell; Yan Gaoqing and his son Yan Jiming were killed in 756. *ChinaKnowledge*
- Yang Guozhong pushed Xuanzong to order Geshu Han out of the pass to attack. **Early July 756** the Tang army was destroyed and Tong Pass lost; Geshu Han was handed to the rebels by his own officer Huoba Guiren (火拔归仁). Chang'an lay open. *ChinaKnowledge; Wikipedia*
- **14 July 756**: Xuanzong left Chang'an for Chengdu. **15 July 756**: at the Mawei post station the escort killed Yang Guozhong and forced Yang Guifei to hang herself. *Wikipedia, Emperor Xuanzong; ChinaKnowledge*
- **12 August 756**: the heir apparent Li Heng, who had refused to go to Sichuan, declared himself emperor at Lingwu; Xuanzong became retired emperor. Li Heng ruled as Suzong. *Wikipedia, Emperor Xuanzong; ChinaKnowledge*
- An Lushan sent Sun Xiaozhe (孙孝哲) to occupy Chang'an while he stayed at Luoyang; his enemies in the capital were massacred. *ChinaKnowledge*
- **29 January 757**: An Lushan was killed in his own bedchamber by his attendant Li Zhu'er, at the instigation of his son An Qingxu and the official Yan Zhuang. An Qingxu took over the Yan state. *Wikipedia, An Lushan; ChinaKnowledge*
- **18 February – 24 November 757**: siege of Suiyang. Zhang Xun and Xu Yuan held the town with about 7,000 men. Suiyang sat on the Tang course of the Grand Canal between Kaifeng and Xuzhou and blocked the rebel route to the Huai and the lower Yangtze. Defenders ate tea leaves, paper and bark; the siege is notorious for large-scale cannibalism. The town fell after nine months and both commanders were killed. *Wikipedia, Siege of Suiyang*
- **13 November 757**: Battle of Xiangji Temple southwest of Chang'an. Tang about 150,000 against Yan about 100,000. Pugu Huai'en, a Tang general of Tiele descent, led 4,000 Uyghur cavalry concealed on the eastern flank; their charge broke the Yan line. Reported casualties, treat as chronicle inflation: 70,000 Tang dead, 60,000 Yan beheaded, 20,000 captured. **14 November 757**: Chang'an retaken. *Wikipedia, Battle of Xiangji Temple; Mackerras*
- **30 November 757**: Guo Ziyi defeated An Qingxu's forces outside Luoyang, again by a Uyghur charge downhill. Tang troops entered Luoyang days later; the Uyghurs plundered it for three days under an agreement made earlier at Chang'an. *Mackerras*
- Suzong had promised the Uyghurs the sack of Chang'an; the prince Li Chu (later Emperor Daizong) talked them into taking Luoyang instead. The Tang paid 20,000 rolls of silk and Uyghur princes received honorary titles. *Mackerras; Wikipedia, Bayanchur Khan*
- An Qingxu executed Geshu Han and some thirty other captive Tang generals as he abandoned Luoyang. *Wikipedia, Geshu Han*
- Shi Siming surrendered 80,000 men, was made Prince Guiyi (归义王) and military commissioner of Fanyang, then defected again when he learned the court distrusted him. *ChinaKnowledge*
- **Autumn 758 – spring 759**: nine military commissions put about 200,000 men under the eunuch army supervisor Yu Chao'en to besiege Yecheng (Xiangzhou). Shi Siming scattered them in spring 759. Theobald attributes the failure to absent unified command rather than rebel strength. *ChinaKnowledge; Mackerras dates the Xiangzhou victory to April 759*
- **10 April 759**: Shi Siming killed An Qingxu, took the Yan throne at Fanyang. **June 760**: he retook Luoyang. **April 761**: his son Shi Chaoyi had him strangled. *Wikipedia, Yan (An–Shi); Mackerras*
- **May 762**: Xuanzong (3 May) and Suzong both died. Shi Chaoyi appealed to the Uyghurs claiming China had no emperor. Bögü Qaghan marched, then switched sides: his qatun was a daughter of Pugu Huai'en, and she arranged a meeting at which her father convinced the qaghan he had been lied to. *Mackerras*
- **20 November 762**: Tang and Uyghur forces beat Shi Chaoyi in the northern suburbs of Luoyang. The plunder that followed was worse than 757. Refugees sheltering in the stupas of two Buddhist temples, one of them the Baima Temple, were burned alive when the Uyghurs fired the buildings. Both standard histories give high casualties. *Mackerras, citing Jiu Tangshu 195 and Xin Tangshu 217A*
- **17 February 763**: Shi Chaoyi, abandoned by his generals at Fanyang, killed himself. The rebellion ended. *Wikipedia, An Lushan rebellion; ChinaKnowledge*
- While in Luoyang from late 762 into 763, Bögü Qaghan met Sogdian Manichaean priests and converted. The Uyghur state became the only polity in East Asia to adopt Manichaeism as its official religion. *Mackerras*

### After the war: what the settlement actually was

- **763 appointments to end the fighting**: Li Huaixian at Lulong (the former Fanyang, seat Beijing), Li Baochen at Chengde (seat Zhengding, Hebei), Tian Chengsi at Weibo (seat Daming, Hebei), Xue Song at Xiangwei (seat Anyang, Henan). All four were former rebel commanders. Tian Chengsi absorbed Xiangwei; the surviving three became the "three garrisons of Hebei" (河北三镇, also 河朔三镇). *ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- These provinces kept their armies, remitted no tax to Chang'an, and asserted a hereditary right to the commissionership. *Wikipedia, Three Fanzhen of Hebei; ChinaKnowledge*
- Hereditary military lineages spread beyond Hebei. Li Zhengji at Ziqing (Pinglu, Shandong) was succeeded by his son Li Na, then grandsons Li Shigu and Li Shidao, a run from 732 to 819. *ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- The Tang response was not to dismantle the system but to extend it: defence commands were created across the whole empire so that one commissioner could check another. Graff counts the ten frontier commands becoming forty-odd provinces after 755, split between loyalist commanders and ex-rebels, and calls the provincial structure the basic framework of Tang military organisation for the rest of the dynasty. *Graff, "The Reach of the Military: Tang"; ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- Graff on the resulting armies: professional, long-service, paid in cash, prone to mutiny over pay, described as a privileged and parasitic caste, and steadily more expensive to keep loyal. *Graff, same*
- **The eunuch armies have a specific origin in this war.** The Shence Army was raised in 754 under Geshu Han as a frontier unit in Longyou against Tibet. Cut off from its home base by the Tibetan advance, it ended up near the capital, helped Daizong recover Chang'an in 763, and became the central palace army under the eunuch Yu Chao'en. *Wikipedia, Shence Army*
- **The western frontier collapsed.** With the Hexi and Longyou armies pulled east, the Tibetan empire took the Gansu corridor and the Central Asian protectorates. **18 November 763**: Tibetan forces entered Chang'an, installed a puppet emperor, and held the city about fifteen days before withdrawing ahead of Guo Ziyi's relief column. Daizong had already fled east on 16 November. *EBSCO Research Starters, "Tibetans Capture Chang'an"; Mackerras*
- **The Uyghur bill was permanent.** After the Qianyuan period (758–760) the Uyghurs sent annual horse embassies, priced at forty pieces of silk per horse, bringing tens of thousands of horses at a time. In September 773 an embassy arrived with about 10,000 horses; Daizong had officials estimate the year's revenue and capped the purchase at 6,000. The Arab traveller Tamīm ibn Bahr, at Karabalghasun around 821, reported the Chinese emperor sending 500,000 pieces of silk a year. Chinese sources put the peak of these payments in 820–830. The Xin Tangshu, in Lu Yu's biography, records the Uyghurs beginning to buy tea with their horses. *Mackerras, citing Jiu Tangshu 195 and Xin Tangshu 217B*
- Uyghur qaghans married six Chinese princesses between 744 and 840, of whom three were daughters of reigning emperors, which was exceptional. Princess Ningguo, Suzong's second daughter, was sent in 758. *Mackerras*
- Pugu Huai'en, who had done more than almost anyone to win the war, rebelled in 764 and again in 765 with Uyghur and Tibetan support. He died in September 765; Guo Ziyi persuaded the Uyghurs to turn on the Tibetans, whom they beat in November 765. That was the last effective Tang-Uyghur military cooperation. *Mackerras*

### The registers: what the numbers are and what they mean

Figures below are from Kent Deng's dataset (LSE working paper 76/03), which draws on Liang Fangzhong's standard compilation and on the Tang sources:

| Year | Registered individuals | Registered households | Persons per household |
|---|---|---|---|
| 742 | 48,909,800 | 8,525,763 | 5.74 |
| 755 | 52,919,309 | 8,914,709 | 5.94 |
| 764 | 16,920,386 | 2,933,125 | 5.80 |

- The commonly quoted pre-war peak is 52.9 million. Some sources date it to 754 (52,880,488) and some to 755 (52,919,309); both round the same way. **The Tang card uses 754. Match it or flag the difference.**
- **The household figure is the tell.** Individuals fell by about 68 per cent, households by about 67 per cent, and average household size barely moved (5.94 to 5.80). Whole households vanished from the books at almost exactly the rate individuals did. Mass mortality thins households; failed registration removes them entirely. *Derived from the Deng table. Present as an observation about the shape of the data, not as a claim attributed to a named scholar.*
- A separate register for 760 gives 2,933,174 households and 16,993,806 individuals, **compiled from only 169 prefectures, well under half the number the Tang had administered before the war.** *Chinese-language demographic summaries drawing on Tongdian and Jiu Tangshu; treat the 169 figure as approximate and attribute loosely.*
- In the same 760 return, "untaxed households" (不课户) were close to two-thirds of the registered total. *Fujian and Shanghai statistics bureau historical summaries drawing on Tongdian*
- Graff: registered households fell from about nine million in 755 to under two million in 760, and the court lost the ability to enrol, count and tax most peasant households directly. Local tax records were destroyed, scattered or made obsolete, and regions collected their own revenue and forwarded a fraction. *Graff, Medieval Chinese Warfare 300–900, as cited in the Quodlibeta analysis*
- Richard Guisso, in the Cambridge History of China, attributes the fall to local authorities simply failing to register the population, and reckons the registers captured far less than half the real population. *Cambridge History of China vol. 3, as cited in the Quodlibeta analysis*
- C. A. Peterson notes large-scale population movement, particularly southward, rather than mass death. *Cambridge History of China vol. 3 ch. 8, as cited in the Quodlibeta analysis*
- **The best single fact for the caveat**: Du You (735–812), the Tang official who compiled the empire's household statistics into the Tongdian (completed 801), thought the Tianbao registers were already wrong. Against 8.9 million registered households at the peak, he estimated the real figure should have been around 13 million, and blamed administrative failure in registration. His chapter "Households through the rise and fall of the ages" (历代盛衰户口) is the first sustained work of Chinese historical demography, and he wrote it as a man who had lived through the rebellion. *Shanghai Municipal Statistics Bureau, "Du You: the art of governing lies in mastering population figures"*
- Mechanics of why the registers were lost: Tang household registers were revised every three years; the original stayed in the prefectural archive, one copy went to the Ministry of Revenue and one back to the district; the Ministry kept them 27 years and local offices 15. The prefectural archives sat in the war zone. *ChinaKnowledge, huji*

### Memory and afterlife

- **Yan Zhenqing, Draft of a Requiem to My Nephew (祭侄文稿), 758.** Written for Yan Jiming, son of Yan Gaoqing, both killed when Changshan fell in 756. Running script on paper, 28.3 × 75.5 cm, 234 characters in 23 columns, heavily struck through and corrected. National Palace Museum, Taipei. Traditionally ranked among the greatest surviving works of Chinese calligraphy precisely because it is a draft written in grief. *Wikimedia Commons file record; HKU Chinese Calligraphy teaching collection*
- **Du Fu (712–770)** wrote "Spring View" (春望) in spring 757 while held in rebel-occupied Chang'an, and the "Three Officers" (三吏) and "Three Partings" (三别) sequences in 758–759, after the rout at Yecheng, on conscription and forced separation. These poems are the reason the war has a human record at all. *Multiple Du Fu scholarly and teaching sources; Columbia University Press, How to Read Chinese Poetry in Context*
- **Bai Juyi (772–846)** wrote the "Song of Everlasting Sorrow" (长恨歌) in 806, about Xuanzong and Yang Guifei and Mawei. It was an immediate popular success and became one of the most memorised poems in the language. His friend Chen Hong wrote a prose companion, the "Account of Everlasting Sorrow" (长恨传). The story then ran through Bai Pu's Yuan drama *Rain on the Paulownia Tree* (梧桐雨) and Hong Sheng's *Palace of Eternal Youth* (长生殿, 1688). *Wikipedia, Chang Hen Ge; World History Encyclopedia*
- **The consequence worth naming**: within fifty years the defining literary treatment of the rebellion was a love story in which the emperor's favourite dies. The military and fiscal catastrophe became a romance, and that is the version most Chinese readers have known since.
- **Sogdians in China concealed their origins afterwards.** The clearest documented case: An Chongzhang (安重璋, 703–777), a Sogdian general from a family long settled at Wuwei, petitioned Suzong in 757 to change his surname because he was ashamed to share it with the rebel. Suzong granted him the surname Li; he is known as Li Baoyu (李抱玉), and the change was applied retroactively to his ancestors. He then spent a decade holding the western frontier against Tibet. *Wikipedia, Li Baoyu*
- More broadly: many Sogdians in China were killed or changed their names after 763, which is why the Sogdian presence in north China becomes hard to trace from that point. *Encyclopaedia Iranica, Sogdian personal names; Smithsonian, The Sogdians*
- The Smithsonian's Sogdian project frames the rebellion as the turning point after which Sogdian communities in China were treated with suspicion, assimilated over a few generations, and lost a distinct identity. *Smithsonian NMAA, "The Sogdians: Influencers on the Silk Roads"*

## Contested

Each item below with the suggested caveat phrasing. The style guide requires at least one; there are several here worth carrying.

1. **The "36 million dead" figure.** Derived by subtracting the 764 register from the 755 register. Matthew White used 36 million in *The Great Big Book of Horrible Things*, and Steven Pinker repeated it in *The Better Angels of Our Nature*, ranking the rebellion among the deadliest events in history. White later revised his own estimate down to 13 million. Historians reject the arithmetic. *Suggested phrasing: the difference between the two registers is often quoted as a death toll of 36 million, which would make this the deadliest event before the twentieth century; the figure measures the loss of registration, and the scholar who popularised it later cut it by two-thirds.*

2. **The census figures generally.** Registers destroyed in the war zone, populations moving beyond the reach of collectors, whole provinces not reporting. The 760 return covered 169 prefectures. Du You thought the pre-war registers already understated households by around a third. *Suggested phrasing: a figure for what the state could still count, not for how many people were alive.* Note that the Tang card already carries a one-clause version of this caveat, so this card should go to the mechanism instead of repeating the clause.

3. **An Lushan's ancestry.** Pulleyblank (1955) and most Western scholarship give a Sogdian father and a Turkic mother of the Ashide clan. Other accounts make the Turkic side dominant and note that the surname An came from his stepfather An Yanyan, which weakens the inference from surname to Sogdian paternity. Wikipedia's own article leads with Göktürk origin and treats the Sogdian father as possible. *Suggested phrasing: described as Sogdian, as Turkic, or as both, depending on which link in the chain of adoption and surname a given scholar trusts.*

4. **The Arab contingent.** Some accounts have the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur sending troops to help the Tang retake the capitals. The number given ranges from 3,000 to 4,000 to over 22,000 depending on the source. The claim rests on references to Dashi (大食) troops in the Chinese chronicles, with no corroboration from Arabic sources. *Suggested phrasing: recommend either omitting or flagging explicitly as a chronicle detail with no independent support. Do not state it as fact.*

5. **The "barbarised Hebei" thesis.** Chen Yinke argued in 1943 that Hebei's post-war autonomy was explained by its ethnocultural transformation into a non-Chinese region, and read Han Yu, Yuan Zhen and Bai Juyi as evidence of rising xenophobia in Chang'an after the rebellion. Shao-yun Yang (Tang Studies 36, 2018) re-examined the key text, Liu Yuxi's 838 stele for Shi Xiaozhang, and argues the "barbarian" language was rhetoric attached to disobedient political behaviour rather than a report of ethnic change. *Suggested phrasing: the standard twentieth-century explanation for Hebei's autonomy was that the region had become culturally non-Chinese; recent work reads the same texts as insults aimed at disloyalty.*

6. **Whether the rebellion destroyed the Tang aristocracy.** The old view, and the one implicit in the Naitō transition thesis, is yes. Nicolas Tackett (*The Destruction of the Medieval Chinese Aristocracy*, 2014) built a database from over 30,000 excavated tomb epitaphs (墓志铭) and argues the great clans came through 755–763 intact, kept the senior posts in the capitals and provinces for another century, and were physically destroyed only in the violence following Huang Chao's sack of the capitals in 880. Tackett also explains the distortion: far more writers of distinction survived the eighth-century war to describe it than survived the ninth-century one. The reviewer Michael Hoeckelmann notes the epitaph sample covers only about 2.59 per cent of an estimated 17,500 capital elite. *Suggested phrasing: this is the single most useful revision to name in the closing section, and it also gives the card a live tension with the Tang card's Naitō paragraph.*

7. **Exact dates in July 756.** The fall of Tong Pass is given as 7 July and as 9 July in different conversions from the lunar record. *Suggested phrasing: early July 756.* Xuanzong's flight (14 July) and Mawei (15 July) are firmer.

8. **Li Linfu's death year.** Given as 752 and as 753. He died in the eleventh lunar month of Tianbao 11, which straddles the Julian year boundary. *Suggested phrasing: the winter of 752–753.*

9. **The Yan state's later era names.** An Lushan's Shengwu (圣武) is well attested. The era names assigned to An Qingxu, Shi Siming and Shi Chaoyi vary between reference sources. *Recommend using only Shengwu, or none.*

10. **Battle casualty numbers.** The Xiangji Temple figures (70,000 Tang dead, 60,000 Yan beheaded) come from the chronicles and follow the usual Chinese historiographical pattern of round inflated numbers. *Suggested phrasing: if used at all, mark as chronicle figures.*

11. **Reading of 李俶.** The future Daizong's given name is usually romanised Lǐ Chù, occasionally Lǐ Tì. He renamed himself Li Yu (李豫) on accession. Safest to call him "the prince who became Emperor Daizong".

12. **吐蕃 reading.** Mainland dictionaries give Tǔbō; Tǔfān is also current. Flag if used.

### New proposals this card generates

**Tier 1**

- `uyghur-khaganate` — 回纥/回鹘, 744–840. The steppe power that decided the war and then charged for it. Nothing in the corpus covers it, and it is needed for `huichang` (Manichaeism), for the horse-silk trade, and for the ninth-century northern frontier.
- `tibetan-empire-tang` — 吐蕃. Took Chang'an in 763 and the whole western corridor. Currently absent from the corpus, which is a large hole for the eighth and ninth centuries.
- `hebei-three-garrisons` — 河北三镇. The concrete form of post-763 autonomy, and the subject of a live scholarly argument (Chen Yinke versus Shao-yun Yang) that no other card can carry.

**Tier 2**

- `guo-ziyi` — 郭子仪. The general who won the war twice and stayed loyal, which is the counter-example to the whole "frontier generals cannot be trusted" reading.
- `yan-zhenqing` — 颜真卿. Calligrapher, loyalist official, and the source of this card's image. Also a natural bridge into a calligraphy card.
- `siege-of-suiyang` — 睢阳. The event where the war's cruelty and its strategic logic meet, and a long-running moral argument in the Chinese tradition about Zhang Xun.
- `changhenge` — 长恨歌. How the rebellion became a love story, running from Bai Juyi to Hong Sheng.
- `du-fu-wartime` — could fold into `li-bai-du-fu`, but the 757–759 poems are a distinct body of evidence about the war.

**Tier 3**

- `manichaeism-china` — 摩尼教, from Bögü Qaghan's conversion at Luoyang in 762 to the 845 suppression. Would connect `an-lushan`, `uyghur-khaganate` and `huichang`.
- `tang-census` — the register system as an institution, since the same numbers problem recurs at Han, Ming and Qing.
- `pugu-huaien` — 仆固怀恩, the loyal general who won the war and then rebelled, a compact illustration of how the settlement failed.
