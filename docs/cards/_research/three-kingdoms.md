## The argument

The Three Kingdoms is the most heavily mythologised period in Chinese history and one of the most thinly documented. The gap between those two facts is the card. What survives is a Wei-centred record, written after the fact by the winners' successors: Chen Shou compiled the standard history under the Jin dynasty that had absorbed Wei, Shu never ran a history office at all, and Wu's own history reached us only in fragments quoted in a fifth-century commentary. On top of that thin base, eight centuries of storytellers, playwrights and finally a Ming novel built the opposite story, in which the weakest of the three states is the legitimate one and the strongest ruler is the villain. The card's job is to hold both: the material reality (Wei held roughly two-thirds of the registered population and still could not finish the job; Shu was a warlord regime in one province that exhausted itself attacking north; Wu was an alliance of southern magnates that outlasted both) against the imaginative afterlife that made this sixty-year stalemate into China's national epic.

A second, quieter argument runs underneath: the period's durable achievement was not military. It was Wu's colonisation of the south. The number of counties in Wu's territory roughly doubled between the Later Han census and the Jin register, which is how the Yangtze valley became governable ground and, from 317, the refuge of the Chinese state. Meanwhile Wei's nine-rank recruitment system, meant to control the great families, ended up institutionalising them, and the Sima family took the throne as the great families' candidate. Both outcomes matter far more for the next four centuries than any battle.

## Facts

### Naming and framing

- 三国 sānguó is conventionally translated "Three Kingdoms", which de Crespigny flags as inaccurate: the three rulers never simultaneously held the title 王 wáng (king/prince). Sun Quan received "King of Wu" from Wei only after Cao Pi and Liu Bei had already declared themselves emperors, and he took the imperial title in 229. "Three states" or Theobald's "Three Empires" is closer. [de Crespigny I, n.36; Theobald]
- The Chinese idiom for the configuration is 三国鼎立 sānguó dǐnglì, three states standing like the three feet of a ritual tripod, also given as 鼎足对峙 dǐngzú duìzhì. [Theobald]
- 220–280 is a bookkeeping frame, not a description of when the fighting started or stopped. The functional division of the empire was complete by early 223, when Sun Quan renounced fealty to Wei and re-negotiated peace with Shu-Han. [de Crespigny I]

### How the Han actually ended

- 24 September 189: Dong Zhuo 董卓 (d. 192), camped outside Luoyang, saw the capital burning as eunuchs and gentry officials killed each other, marched in, and effectively ended imperial authority. de Crespigny treats this as the precise fall of the Han, thirty-one years before the formal abdication. [de Crespigny I]
- 28 September 189: Dong Zhuo deposed the boy emperor Liu Bian 刘辩 and enthroned his half-brother Liu Xie 刘协 (181–234), known as Emperor Xian 汉献帝 (r. 189–220). Liu Xie remained a puppet of successive warlords for thirty-one years.
- 184: the Yellow Turban uprising, led by Zhang Jiao 张角 (d. 184) of the Way of Great Peace 太平道. Separately, Zhang Lu 张鲁 (d. 216) ran a theocratic government in Hanzhong 汉中 commandery as leader of the Way of the Five Pecks of Rice 五斗米道, recognised by the modern Daoist church as third patriarch of the Celestial Masters 天师. **Note:** Theobald's ChinaKnowledge page mistakenly places Zhang Jiao's rising in Hanzhong. It was in the east (Hebei, Shandong, Henan). Hanzhong was Zhang Lu. Do not repeat that error.
- 191: Sun Jian 孙坚 (c. 155–192), under Yuan Shu, drove Dong Zhuo from Luoyang to Chang'an. 192: Dong Zhuo assassinated by his bodyguard Lü Bu 吕布.
- 196: Cao Cao 曹操 (155–220) took custody of Emperor Xian and set up an imperial court at Xu 许 (present-day Xuchang 许昌, Henan), governing as a loyal minister of Han while building a parallel administration.
- 197: Yuan Shu 袁术 declared himself emperor at Shouchun 寿春. Even his allies rejected the claim; abandoned, he died in summer 199. The imperial title was still understood to belong to the Liu house. [de Crespigny I]
- Summer 200: battle of Guandu 官渡, near present-day Kaifeng. Cao Cao, heavily outnumbered, held a defensive line for over a month; a raid on Yuan Shao's supply train broke the northern army's morale. Yuan Shao died 202.
- 204: Cao Cao took Ye 邺 (present-day Linzhang 临漳, Hebei), which became his administrative centre.
- 207: campaign beyond the frontier against the Wuhuan 乌桓, ending at White Wolf Mountain. By the end of 207 Cao Cao governed roughly half the empire's population and held the whole North China plain.
- 213: Emperor Xian made Cao Cao Duke of Wei 魏公 with the Nine Bestowments 九锡, capital at Ye. 216: promoted to King of Wei 魏王, fief of 30,000 households. 217: named Cao Pi heir.
  **Caution:** de Crespigny's 1991 article gives 214 and 217 for these two promotions; the standard dating from the Sanguozhi (Jian'an 18 and Jian'an 21) is 213 and 216. Use 213/216.

### Red Cliffs, 208

- Fought winter 208–209 (Jian'an 13), on the Yangtze between present-day Wuhan and Yueyang.
- Cao Cao publicly claimed 800,000 men. Zhou Yu's assessment recorded in the Sanguozhi put the real figure at roughly 220,000–240,000, of which 70,000–80,000 were newly surrendered Jing province troops of doubtful loyalty. The allies fielded roughly 50,000 under Zhou Yu 周瑜 (175–210) and Cheng Pu, about 30,000 from Sun Quan and 20,000 from Liu Bei.
- Cao Cao's northern troops had marched hard, had no experience of southern waterways, and sickness was reported in camp. Much of his fleet had previously served Liu Biao 刘表 (d. 208) of Jing province.
- Huang Gai 黄盖 used a feigned surrender to bring fire ships downwind into Cao Cao's chained fleet. Fire was an ordinary weapon of the period, not an exotic stratagem.
- de Crespigny's reading: Cao Cao probably treated the operation as an armed reconnaissance, the scale of his defeat was exaggerated at the time and grossly inflated afterwards, and yet the outcome was decisive, because control of the middle Yangtze was the difference between southern survival and southern surrender.
- Location is genuinely unsettled. At least seven candidate sites have been proposed. The most widely accepted is present-day Chibi City 赤壁市 in Hubei, which was called Puqi 蒲圻 until it was renamed in 1998 for tourism. Wulin 乌林 lies opposite on the north bank.

### Consolidation, 209–222

- 209: Zhou Yu took Jiangling 江陵. 210: Zhou Yu died; Sun Quan had no equivalent commander and transferred Jiangling to Liu Bei.
- 211: Cao Cao broke the warlord coalition in the Wei valley and reoccupied Chang'an.
- 214: Liu Bei 刘备 (161–223), invited into Yi province 益州 by Liu Zhang 刘璋 to help against enemies to the north, turned on his host and took Chengdu 成都.
- 215: Cao Cao forced Zhang Lu's surrender in Hanzhong. Sun Quan sent Lü Meng 吕蒙 (178–219) against the southern Jing commanderies; Guan Yu 关羽 counter-attacked; the Xiang River became the boundary.
- Early 219: Liu Bei defeated and killed Cao Cao's general Xiahou Yuan 夏侯渊 in Hanzhong, secured his northern border and proclaimed himself King of Hanzhong.
- Summer/autumn 219: Guan Yu attacked up the Han River, was helped by heavy flooding which washed away a Wei army in the open, and besieged Fan 樊 near Xiangyang 襄阳 for three months.
- Autumn 219: Lü Meng's surprise attack west along the Yangtze took Jiangling. Guan Yu's position collapsed; he was killed and most of his army surrendered. This is the hinge of the whole period: it fixed Shu inside Sichuan permanently.
- 221–222: Liu Bei's revenge expedition down the Yangtze. Lu Xun 陆逊 (183–245) let him overextend, then attacked his flank with fire in the sixth Chinese month. Shu's army was broken (battle of Yiling 夷陵 / Xiaoting). Liu Bei fled to Baidi 白帝 city near present-day Fengjie and died there in 223.
- Winter 222 / beginning of 223: Sun Quan renounced Wei suzerainty, defeated Cao Pi's punitive expedition on both the middle and lower Yangtze, declared his own reign title, and re-made peace with Shu. The three-way division was now militarily complete. [de Crespigny I]

### The three foundations, with dates

| State | Founder | Formal date | Capital | End |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wei 曹魏 | Cao Pi 曹丕 (187–226) | 11 December 220, abdication of Emperor Xian | Luoyang 洛阳 (with Xu 许 and Ye 邺 as the other two chief cities) | Sima Yan takes the throne, 4 February 266 |
| Shu-Han 蜀汉 | Liu Bei | 221 (see contested dates) | Chengdu 成都 | Liu Shan surrenders, winter 263 |
| Wu 孙吴 | Sun Quan 孙权 (182–252) | King of Wu from Wei 221; independent reign title 222; imperial title 229 | Wuchang 武昌 222–229 and 265–266; Jianye 建业 (present-day Nanjing) 229–265 and 266–280 | Sun Hao surrenders, 1 May 280 |

- Cao Pi's first reign title was Huangchu 黄初 ("Yellow Beginning"), marking the succession of the power of Earth (yellow) to the Fire and red of Han. Sun Quan chose Huangwu 黄武 in 222 and Huanglong 黄龙 in 229, both also yellow. [de Crespigny I, n.90]
- Shu-Han is named for an old name for the Sichuan region; the court itself simply called the state Han 汉.
- Three grounds of legitimacy, all incompatible: Wei by formal abdication (禅让 shànràng) plus control of the heartland; Shu-Han by Liu Bei's claimed descent from the Former Han imperial house; Wu by the argument that the imperial position had been vacated and no worthy successor had filled it, that Sun Quan's own government was virtuous, and that the Cao were criminal usurpers. Sun Quan's proclamation, preserved in Pei Songzhi's commentary quoting the *Wu lu*, simply declared the Han succession irrelevant. No traditional historian has ever accepted his claim. [de Crespigny I]

### Wei: the strongest state

- Population share: Wei held roughly two-thirds of the old empire's registered population by the Later Han figures, and de Crespigny elsewhere states "some seventy per cent". Wu held about one-sixth. [de Crespigny I]
- 263 surrender figures for Shu and 280 for Wu are recorded; Wei's own registered total is usually given as 663,423 households and 4,432,881 individuals around 263, a figure Wikipedia flags as disputed. Treat with caution.
- **Tuntian 屯田, agricultural garrisons.** Established from about 196 around Xu, on land abandoned by refugees. The distinctive feature was a direct relationship between farmer and state: the government supplied land, tools and plough oxen and took a share of the crop. Cao Cao's adviser Zao Zhi 枣祗 argued successfully for a percentage of yield rather than a fixed rent, on the grounds that share-cropping rewards higher production. The rate settled at 50% of annual yield, or 60% where the oxen were state-owned. Ren Jun 任峻 (d. 204) was the first Commissioner for Agriculture, ranked equivalent to a commandery Grand Administrator. Colonies answered to the central or provincial government that founded them, not to the commandery they sat in. Tenants were exempt from general corvée and from military conscription; their job was food. [de Crespigny I; Theobald]
- Administration ran through 典农中郎将 diǎnnóng zhōnglángjiàng at the centre, with 典农校尉 and 典农都尉 below. Later, military colonies were supervised by 度支中郎将 dùzhī zhōnglángjiàng. [Theobald]
- Comparative scale: the colonies produced only a fraction of the resources of Cao Cao's territory. He still had to negotiate with local centres of power. [de Crespigny I]
- Liu Fu 刘馥 (d. 208) set up agricultural garrisons around Shouchun 寿春 and Hefei 合肥 as a settled peasant militia; these gave the Huai frontier long-term stability and Sun Quan never broke it.
- 243: Deng Ai 邓艾 (c. 200–264) memorialised for a major extension of garrisons in the Huai valley, men serving as both farmers and soldiers, to stockpile grain for an offensive against Wu.
- **The nine-rank system 九品中正制 jiǔpǐn zhōngzhèngzhì.** Han recruitment worked by local recommendation, mostly in the category "Filially Pious and Incorrupt" 孝廉 xiàolián, and had fallen into the hands of local gentry by the late second century, then collapsed entirely in the civil war. Cao Cao's replacement appointed Rectifiers 中正 zhōngzhèng to each commandery, specialists who graded candidates into nine categories with a written character assessment. All offices were separately graded into nine ranks, with no necessary correlation between a man's category and his current post; the category was a judgement of potential. Unlike Han practice, Rectifiers supervised their own home territory, though they were commissioned from the capital. Grade one appears never to have been awarded, being reserved notionally for the emperor.
- Attribution is contested. de Crespigny says Cao Cao instituted it and the structure was established under Cao Pi in 220. A separate tradition (the *Taiping yulan* quoting Fu Xuan 傅玄, 217–278) credits Chen Qun 陈群 (d. 236) as Minister of Personnel; Chen Qun's own Sanguozhi biography does not mention it. Theobald credits Cao Cao. Wikipedia and most reference works credit Chen Qun under Cao Pi in 220.
- What it actually did: Liu Yi 刘毅 (d. 285) complained in a celebrated Western Jin memorial that no man of humble family reached the highest categories and no member of a powerful clan appeared in the lower ranks. Tanigawa Michio's reading, quoted by de Crespigny: the Nine Ranks system was the concrete institutional form in which the Six Dynasties aristocracy established itself as a ruling class. The system meant to satisfy the great families while limiting them ended up entrenching them. It survived, modified, until the Tang. [de Crespigny I; Theobald; Wikipedia]
- **Talent over character.** Cao Cao issued edicts in 210, 214 and 217 (求贤令 qiúxiánlìng, "seeking talent") asking that men of ability be recommended regardless of personal conduct or background. de Crespigny frames the underlying conflict as Cao Cao's Legalist/"Modernist" push for strong central government against the great clans' "Confucian"/"Reformist" defence of family status and local autonomy.
- **Hereditary military households.** 士家 shìjiā / 军户 jūnhù, recorded in separate military registers 士籍. The system grew out of the practice of taking hostages 质任 from commanders: relatives held at Ye while the man served at the front, then extended to ordinary troops. Under Cao Pi there were reportedly 100,000 military households, half moved to Luoyang. Sons replaced fathers, younger brothers replaced older; defection was punished by killing the family; daughters of military households could only marry into other military households, because female infanticide threatened the supply of soldiers. [Theobald]
- **Fiscal.** Cao Cao levied a household tax 户调 hùdiào in silk and floss, reduced land tax to a thirtieth of the harvest, and tried and failed to restore the wushu 五铢 coinage after Dong Zhuo's debasement destroyed the money economy in the 190s. In 221 Wei formally declared grain and silk the official media of exchange. [Theobald; de Crespigny II]
- **Culture.** The Jian'an 建安 era (196–220) produced the literary flowering associated with the Cao family and the Seven Masters of Jian'an 建安七子, gathered at Cao Cao's court. Cao Cao was a serious poet; Cao Pi wrote criticism; Cao Zhi 曹植 (192–232) is ranked among China's great poets. In 210 Cao Cao built the Copper Bird Terrace 铜雀台 on the walls of Ye; Cao Zhi, then eighteen, wrote a rhapsody for the occasion.
- A major epidemic in 217 killed several of the Seven Masters (Xu Gan, Chen Lin, Ying Yang, Liu Zhen), with Wang Can 王粲 (177–217) dying on campaign the same year. de Crespigny notes it was recorded across the empire and was the last of the pestilences that had afflicted China since the 170s. Cao Pi's "Letter to Wu Zhi" and "Discourse on Literature" mourn them.
- **Frontier.** 238: Sima Yi 司马懿 (179–251) destroyed the Gongsun 公孙 warlord state in Liaodong, capturing Xiangping. 244–245: Guanqiu Jian 毌丘俭 broke Koguryŏ 高句丽. Embassies from the Wa ruler Himiko 卑弥呼 of Yamatai reached the Wei court; the Chinese account is in the "Account of the Wa people" in Sanguozhi 30, the foundational text for early Japanese history. Cao Cao brought the last Southern Xiongnu Shanyu 单于 to his court as a hostage and organised the Xiongnu into five divisions; Wei abandoned any claim beyond the Ordos and the Sanggan River. de Crespigny's assessment: the north-east campaigns were energetic but left a vacuum on the frontier that allowed the Murong 慕容 Xianbei and others to build the power that would break the north in the fourth century.

### Shu-Han: a warlord regime in one province

- Liu Bei came from Zhuo 涿 commandery near present-day Beijing, made his first reputation fighting Yellow Turbans in 184, then served or allied with one warlord after another for two decades. His force was destroyed by a detachment of Cao Cao's army around Guandu in 200 and he took refuge with Liu Biao.
- de Crespigny's characterisation: Shu-Han was the work of an itinerant warlord entrepreneur and the soldiers of fortune who followed him, and it never became anything more than a provincial warlord state with imperial pretensions.
- Zhuge Liang 诸葛亮 (181–234) acted as regent from Liu Bei's death in 223, when Liu Shan 刘禅 (207–271) was seventeen. He confirmed the Wu alliance and campaigned south in 224–225, reaching Dian Lake 滇池 in present-day Yunnan.
- The southern settlement left the defeated peoples under their own leaders, confirmed with seals of authority. There were later rebellions, and the south added little to Shu's resources. Contrast with Wu: counties in Wu's territory doubled between the Later Han census and the Jin register (160 to 322); in Shu's territory the net gain was about 20%, with no real development programme. [de Crespigny I]
- 227: Zhuge Liang moved his headquarters to Hanzhong and submitted the Chushi biao 出师表 to Liu Shan. Five northern expeditions across the Qin Ling 秦岭 range, 228–234. 228: defeat at Jieting 街亭, after which Zhuge Liang executed Ma Su 马谡 (190–228) and demoted himself. 229: gained Wudu 武都 commandery. 234: died at Wuzhangyuan 五丈原, opposed by Sima Yi.
- Succession of chief ministers: Jiang Wan 蒋琬 (d. 246), Fei Yi 费祎 (assassinated 251 by a defector from Wei), then the general Jiang Wei 姜维 (202–264), who spent a decade trying to break the Qin Ling line, partly through alliance with the Qiang 羌.
- Liu Shan took formal authority at Chengdu in 246 aged forty and was, on de Crespigny's reading, probably fairly accused of preferring his harem to government; he gave excessive power to his favourite Chen Zhi 陈祗 and the eunuch Huang Hao 黄皓.
- Administratively minimal. The office of Chancellor lapsed on Zhuge Liang's death. Senior officials held military titles and were usually at the front; civil affairs at Chengdu were left to Liu Shan and his favourites. Chen Shou, himself a Shu official, wrote in his appraisal of Liu Shan that the state employed no historians and had no office responsible for collecting records, so much information was simply lost.
- Army size: a figure of 250,000 infantry and cavalry appears for 227 but is too large for Sichuan's population; roughly 140,000 early and about 100,000 at the end is the modern estimate. After Yiling, 5,000 troops were drafted from 12,000 households. Effectively every adult male in Shu served at some point. [Theobald, citing He 1987]
- Autumn 263: Wei took the passes into Hanzhong; one army pinned Jiang Wei while Deng Ai marched on Chengdu. Liu Shan surrendered that winter after a single pitched battle. Deng Ai was then dismissed and assassinated, and Jiang Wei and the Wei commander Zhong Hui 钟会 (225–264) tried to set up an independent regime; both were killed. Summer 264: Liu Shan taken to honoured exile at Luoyang.
- 263 surrender count: 280,000 households, 940,000 individuals, 40,000 officials, 102,000 men under arms. For comparison, Yi province under Later Han had a registered population of 7,250,000, and the three commanderies around Chengdu alone had more than 575,000 households and 2,250,000 individuals. de Crespigny's reading: the Jin geographical treatise gives just over 300,000 households for the same area twenty years later, which is compatible with the Shu figure and indicates a tax quota system rather than a real collapse in the number of people living there.

### Wu: the state that changed the map

- The Sun family came from Wu 吴 commandery in present-day Zhejiang and were not a leading local family. Sun Jian made his career as a fighting soldier; his son Sun Ce 孙策 (175–200) took service with Yuan Shu at about eighteen, declared independence at twenty-three in 198, and by 200 held Danyang 丹阳, Wu, Kuaiji 会稽, Yuzhang 豫章 and Lujiang 庐江, from present-day Nanjing past Hangzhou Bay with outposts on the Fujian coast. He was assassinated in 200 by retainers of a defeated rival. Sun Quan, then eighteen, took over.
- The demographic precondition: the four commanderies Sun Quan initially held had under two million registered people at the end of Former Han and more than three million by the mid-second century. de Crespigny treats this Han-period population growth south of the Yangtze as the critical enabling fact for the division of the empire after 220. Under the Later Han census, the southern parts of Jing and Yang provinces held about seven million, and Jiao 交 in the far south perhaps a million and a half.
- Expansion was the state's survival strategy. He Qi 贺齐 (d. 227) and Lu Xun pushed conquest and colonisation into present-day southern Zhejiang, Jiangxi and Fujian. In 234 Zhuge Ke 诸葛恪 (203–253) led a final assault on the hill people of Danyang. The pattern was mostly small-scale, persistent local aggression backed by official force when needed. Each advance registered new taxpayers and conscripts, who financed the next advance.
- The far south: 210 Sun Quan appointed Bu Zhi 步骘 (d. 248) governor of Jiao, based at Panyu 番禺 (present-day Guangzhou). Shi Xie 士燮 (137–226), a Chinese-descended magnate whose capital Longbian 龙编 near present-day Hanoi was a major trading centre and a refuge from the northern wars, sent tribute and hostages. When Shi Xie died in 226, Lü Dai 吕岱 (161–256) picked a quarrel with his sons, executed them and sent their heads to Sun Quan. Wu's authority then ran to present-day Cambodia and it took over the Red River delta's seaborne trade.
- Maritime ventures: 230, a fleet of 10,000 men under Wei Wen 卫温 and Zhuge Zhi 诸葛直 sent to find Yizhou 夷洲 and Danzhou 亶洲, over the objections of Lu Xun and Quan Cong. Danzhou was not found. The force returned in 231 with several thousand captives, having lost the great majority of its men to disease; both commanders were executed. Yizhou is usually identified as Taiwan, though not securely. Wu envoys Zhu Ying 朱应 and Kang Tai 康泰 travelled to Funan 扶南 and Southeast Asia in the mid-third century; Kang Tai's *Wushi waiguo zhuan* 吴时外国传 and Zhu Ying's account are the earliest Chinese descriptions of Funan and survive only in quotation.
- Structure of power: an alliance of powerful local families under the hegemony of the Sun clan, with civilian authority closely subordinated to military power. Deng Ai observed in 253 that the great families of Wu, relying on their military strength and their private retainers, held the real power of the state. Commanders held troops and passed them to their sons; a system of internal hostages 保质 baǒzhì kept the magnates in line, but they could not be dismantled. de Crespigny divides Wu's history in two: an energetic warlord enterprise under Sun Ce and the young Sun Quan, then, once expansion stalled in the 220s, a coalition of magnates concerned to preserve their own wealth.
- Private military units 部曲 bùqū ran from several hundred to four or five thousand men and defended fortified manors 坞堡 wùbǎo. Estimates put roughly 60% of Wu's army as impressed Shanyue 山越 hill people and men from the resisting local clans 宗部 zōngbù. Around 250 Wu's army stood at roughly 230,000 with more than 5,000 vessels. [Theobald, citing He 1987]
- Succession crisis: heir Sun Deng 孙登 died 241; Sun He 孙和 deposed 250; Sun Quan died 252 leaving the seven-year-old Sun Liang 孙亮 under Zhuge Ke's guardianship. Zhuge Ke assassinated 253 after a disastrous attack on Hefei. Sun Jun 孙峻, then Sun Lin 孙綝, then Sun Liang deposed 258 and replaced by Sun Xiu 孙休, who died 264, the same year Shu fell. Sun Hao 孙皓 (d. 283/284) chosen as an adult ruler who might restore the state's fortunes; he did not.
- 280 surrender count: 523,000 households, 2,300,000 individuals, 32,000 officials, 230,000 men under arms. Pei Songzhi, quoting the fourth-century *Jin yangqiu*, adds stores of 2,800,000 hu of grain (about 56 million litres), more than 5,000 ships, and 5,000 women in the imperial harem, in 43 commanderies and 313 counties.

### The Sima takeover and the fall

- Sima Yi came from a respected, long-established family in Henei 河内 commandery, entered Cao Cao's service in 208 and became a personal friend of Cao Pi. First field command 226, in his late forties, after a purely civil and administrative career.
- Cao Pi died in 226 aged forty. His son Cao Rui 曹叡 (204–239) came to the throne at twenty-two with four regent advisers: Chen Qun, Cao Zhen 曹真, Cao Xiu 曹休 and Sima Yi. Within a few years the other three were dead.
- Cao Pi had deliberately stripped the imperial clan of power: brothers and half-brothers dispatched to their fiefs, watched, shuffled between titles, barred from government. He also barred empress dowagers and consort families from any role in government, and consorts were chosen from families too obscure to rival the Cao. The policy removed two classic sources of court trouble and left the throne with no allies when it needed them. [de Crespigny I]
- Cao Rui died 238 aged thirty-five with no sons; the throne went to the seven-year-old Cao Fang 曹芳 under Cao Shuang 曹爽 and Sima Yi.
- The Zhengshi 正始 reign period (240–249), Cao Shuang's regency, was a moment of intellectual brilliance: the Study of the Mysteries 玄学 xuánxué, He Yan 何晏 (d. 249) and Wang Bi 王弼 (226–249), one of the great interpreters of the Book of Changes, and the culture of "pure conversation" 清谈 qīngtán. He Yan and his circle were also known as libertines and users of the Five Minerals Powder 五石散 wǔshísàn (also "Cold-eating Powder" 寒食散), a stimulant of ground stalactite and aconite. The associated figures Ruan Ji 阮籍 (210–263) and Xi Kang 嵇康 (223–262), of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove 竹林七贤, are part of the same movement. Sima Yi was able to present himself as the representative of respectable families wanting Confucian restraint. [de Crespigny I]
- Early 249: with the emperor and Cao Shuang out at the dynastic tombs, Sima Yi staged a coup, seized the party and massacred Cao Shuang, his colleagues and their kinsmen. Wei was in Sima hands from then on.
- 251 Sima Yi died; 255 Sima Shi 司马师 (208–255) died after crushing Guanqiu Jian's rising at Shouchun; Sima Zhao 司马昭 (211–265) succeeded. 254: Cao Fang deposed, Cao Mao 曹髦 (241–260) installed. 257–258: Zhuge Dan 诸葛诞 rebelled at Shouchun and sought Wu's help; the city was retaken. Both southern risings failed because Wu could not deliver support.
- 260: Cao Mao led a conspiracy in person and was killed in the fighting. Cao Huan 曹奂 (246–302) became the last puppet.
- 263 Shu conquered. 264 Sima Zhao took the title King of Jin. Autumn 265 Sima Zhao died; his son Sima Yan 司马炎 (236–290) received Cao Huan's abdication on 4 February 266 in a ceremony deliberately echoing Cao Pi's forty-five years earlier.
- de Crespigny's verdict on why the Sima won: the Cao were a dynasty of marginal lineage still close to its military origins; after Cao Pi's early death no Cao emperor took command of the army; and the government tried to demonstrate legitimacy through display rather than action. Men of good family recognised Sima Yi and his sons as their own kind and their own interests.
- 264 (Wei, under Sima Zhao) and again 266 (Jin): decrees abolishing the separate administration of the agricultural garrisons, "in order to equalise the corvée services". de Crespigny reads this as a concession to the powerful families to whom the Sima owed the throne, abandoning the centralising instrument Cao Cao had built.
- 279–280: the conquest of Wu. Wang Jun 王濬 (206–285), Inspector of Yi province, built a fleet in Sichuan and sailed through the Yangtze gorges, breaking the river barriers. Five further armies attacked from the north; roughly 200,000 men in total. Sun Hao surrendered on 1 May 280. The plan had been urged for years by Yang Hu 羊祜 (221–278), then by Du Yu 杜预 and the minister Zhang Hua 张华.
- Jin's post-conquest land settlement: an allocation system 课田 kètián (50 mu for a regular male aged 16–60, half for a secondary male, 20 mu for a regular female) and a registration law 占田 zhàntián (70 mu per male, 30 per female) intended to cap holdings. de Crespigny, following Crowell, treats kètián as a real allocation carried out in places and zhàntián as a statement of principle for later enforcement, never enforced. Tax was assessed per household in silk and floss.

### Population: the headline number and why it is not what it looks like

- Later Han treatise figures, from about 140: roughly 9.7 million households and just under 50 million individuals. The often-quoted 157 CE census gives 10,677,960 households and 56,486,856 individuals.
- Jin figures of about 280: roughly 2.5 million households and 16 million individuals. The commonly cited exact figures are 2,459,840 households and 16,163,863 individuals.
- That is a nominal drop of roughly 70%, and it is the single most repeated statistic about the period.
- Hans Bielenstein showed that the household numbers in the *Jin shu* geographical treatise are a taxation list, not a census, best understood as quotas indicating each unit's assessed obligation, with only incidental relation to the number of people actually living there. The Han figures were a real census; the two are not comparable. [de Crespigny II, citing Bielenstein, "The Census of China during the Period 2–742 AD"]
- de Crespigny's conclusion: the wars unquestionably killed large numbers in the central heartland and depopulated the northern frontier, but the loss was certainly not as great as the figures suggest, and against it must be set the very real growth of registered settlement south of the Yangtze. The recorded figures represent the people the administration could reach for corvée and conscription; the rest avoided registration by distance or by sheltering under a great family.
- The same pattern shows in the individual surrender counts for Shu (940,000) and Wu (2,300,000), both far below the Later Han registers for the same territory.
- The card should give the number and immediately give the caveat. This is the model the Tang card already sets with the 52.9 to 16.9 million figure.

### The record itself

- The *Sanguozhi* 三国志 was compiled by Chen Shou 陈寿 (233–297), a native of Shu who served the Shu court, then served Jin as an official historian after 263. 65 juan: Book of Wei 30, Book of Wu 20, Book of Shu 15. Roughly 360,000 characters. Organised entirely as biographies. It has no treatises and no tables, which is why almost all institutional information about the period has to be reconstructed from elsewhere.
- Its structural bias is explicit: Wei rulers get imperial annals and the title "Emperor"; Shu and Wu rulers are "Lords" or are named personally. Chen Shou dated Wei's establishment to 220.
- Shu had no history office. The Sui shu bibliography states that of the three states only Wei and Wu had officers in charge of history. Chen Shou's own appraisal of Liu Shan says the same. The Book of Shu was written from Chen Shou's personal notes and material he collected himself. [Tian; Wikipedia]
- Wu's *Wu shu* 吴书 was begun in Sun Quan's last years by Ding Fu 丁孚 and Xiang Jun 项峻, then taken over after 252 by Wei Zhao 韦昭 (204–273, renamed Wei Yao 韦曜 to avoid a Jin taboo) with Hua He 华覈, Xue Ying 薛莹 and others. Sun Hao imprisoned Wei Zhao in 273 and had him executed despite Hua He's plea that the encomia were still unwritten. The work does not survive whole, but Pei Songzhi quotes it repeatedly, and those quotations are the only place a Wu perspective on events survives. [Tian]
- Wei commissioned its own *Wei shu* 魏书 in 255 from Wang Chen 王沈 (d. 266), Xun Yi and the poet Ruan Ji, quite possibly as a countermove to Wu's project. It was judged to have made many concessions to contemporary taboos.
- **Cao Man zhuan 曹瞒传**, "Biography of Cao the Trickster", is an anonymous Wu account of Cao Cao's life, quoted extensively by Pei Songzhi. It portrays him as cunning, informal, theatrical, cruel and terrifying, and many of the anecdotes are found nowhere else. Tian's point: these enemy-state anecdotes became character-defining and fed directly into the late imperial villain Cao Cao. This is one of the strongest single facts in the brief.
- **Pei Songzhi 裴松之 (372–451)** completed his annotation in 429 under the Liu Song. He collected parallel and contradictory accounts, corrected the text, and commented on both events and Chen Shou's judgements. Estimates of how much he added range from doubling to tripling the length. The annotated text is the standard one. Without Pei, most of what survives of Shu and Wu writing would be gone.
- Textual survival was shaped by later canon-making. Tian's argument: fifth- and sixth-century men of letters treated Wei as the orthodox literary lineage, because the Southern Dynasties were heirs of the Western Jin that had replaced Wei. The sixth-century *Wen xuan* 文选 anthologises exactly two pieces by Shu or Wu writers, Zhuge Liang's Chushi biao and Wei Zhao's "Disquisition on Liubo and Chess" 博弈论, while including several Wei pieces attacking Wu and Shu. Large numbers of Wu literary collections were still extant when it was compiled and were not chosen. The canonisation of the Jian'an/Cao-Wei group is a major reason the Shu and Wu textual record was lost, and that loss then distorts what modern scholars can see. J. Michael Farmer's related observation, quoted by Tian: the modern emphasis on Wei perpetuates a traditional bias against southern culture.
- Wu writers were unusually active in writing histories of the **Eastern Han**, because Wu presented itself as Han's rightful heir. Xie Cheng 谢承 (182–254), Sun Quan's brother-in-law, wrote a *Hou Han shu*; Wei Zhao's *Dong ji* 洞纪 ran from the beginnings of Chinese history to 222.

### The legitimacy argument, and how it flipped

- Chen Shou, though a man of Shu, treated Wei as Han's heir. Two reasons: Wei was much the strongest power, and it held the Central Plains, the "middle" that Han-era theory (Dong Zhongshu) required a legitimate power to occupy. His position appears to reflect what was normal opinion in his own time. [Wang]
- **Xi Zuochi 习凿齿 (d. 384)**, an Eastern Jin official-historian, attacked that view in the *Han Jin chunqiu* 汉晋春秋. His argument was that Wei had failed to reunify and had usurped Han, so it was a political arriviste; Jin was different because it both unified the realm and ended Wei's usurpation. The consequence, which mattered more than the intention, was that he elevated Shu-Han, because Liu Bei was of the Han royal house. Andrew Chittick's caution, cited by Wang: promoting Shu was probably not Xi Zuochi's primary aim; he was arguing about righteousness as a criterion.
- Rao Zongyi 饶宗颐 (1917–2018), who wrote the comprehensive study of the legitimation debate, held that Xi Zuochi's thesis had little influence until after the Tang, and Wang agrees: few followed it while China was unified.
- The debate reignited in the Song, when the dynasty held far less territory than the Tang and, after 1127, had lost the north entirely. **Ouyang Xiu 欧阳修 (1007–1072)** was among the first to notice Xi Zuochi's position; he required both justice and unity for legitimacy. **Sima Guang 司马光 (1019–1086)** was dismissive of the whole debate, counted only Zhou, Qin, Han, Jin, Sui and Tang as legitimate, called the rest "arrayed states" 列国 lièguó, and nonetheless used **Wei's** reign years in the *Zizhi tongjian* 资治通鉴 to keep a continuous calendar, which his contemporaries and successors called inconsistent.
  **Do not repeat the common error** that Sima Guang compiled the *Tongjian gangmu*. He compiled the *Zizhi tongjian*.
- **Zhu Xi 朱熹 (1130–1200)**, born three years after the Song lost the north, openly disagreed with Sima Guang and made **Shu** the legitimate line, on the principle that what a ruler does matters and where he does it does not. The recension associated with him, the *Zizhi tongjian gangmu* 资治通鉴纲目, carries that judgement. In later life he qualified the position, calling Shu holder of only the "residue of legitimacy" 正统之余 zhèngtǒng zhī yú.
- The pattern is worth stating plainly: the choice of who was legitimate in the third century tracks the political anxieties of the century doing the choosing. A dynasty that had lost the north preferred the state whose claim did not depend on holding the north. de Crespigny notes the same reading of "a true government maintained on Chinese soil but exiled from the traditional centre of power" was popular again in the Republic of China on Taiwan.

### The afterlife: from history to epic

- Su Shi 苏轼 (1037–1101) recorded in *Dongpo zhilin* 东坡志林 that children given money to go and hear street storytellers wept when Liu Bei was defeated and cheered when Cao Cao was. He drew a moral about the durability of a gentleman's influence. This shows the pro-Shu sympathy was already fixed in popular narrative by the Northern Song, well before any novel. By 1117 the storytellers were an organised guild in Kaifeng, later Hangzhou. [Idema and West, *Battles, Betrayals, and Brotherhood*, as reported]
- The *Sanguozhi pinghua* 三国志平话, a storyteller's prompt-book, dates to about 1321–1323 under the Yuan. A large body of Yuan zaju 杂剧 drama on Three Kingdoms subjects preceded the novel.
- The *Sanguo yanyi* 三国演义 (also *Sanguozhi tongsu yanyi* 三国志通俗演义) is attributed to Luo Guanzhong 罗贯中 (c. 1315 – early Ming), about whom almost nothing is securely known. The main evidence is the sequel to the *Lugui bu* 录鬼簿, rediscovered in 1931, which describes him as a native of Taiyuan who wrote three plays; other evidence places him near Hangzhou in the mid-1360s.
- The earliest surviving printed edition is the Jiajing edition of 1522, in 24 juan, which credits Chen Shou for the history and Luo Guanzhong for the compilation. A 1494 preface by Jiang Daqi 蒋大器 makes the same attribution, though it may have derived its details from the text itself. Later commentary editions dropped the author credit entirely. The **Mao Zonggang 毛宗岗** recension of the 1660s–70s, made with his father Mao Lun 毛纶 just after the fall of the Ming, arranged the text into 120 chapters and is the version almost everyone now reads. Scholars accept the Luo attribution cautiously, largely for want of a motive to invent it: he was neither a famous historian nor a celebrated dramatist. [BabelStone]
- The novel inverts Chen Shou. Cao Cao becomes the usurper, Liu Bei the rightful heir, Zhuge Liang a strategist with supernatural powers, Guan Yu a red-faced demigod. de Crespigny's flat judgement: the novel and the dramas are not independent sources, they are frequently and sometimes deliberately wrong, and they are worth no more for third-century history than Shakespeare's plays are for the history of England or Rome.
- Concrete example of the gap, worth using: chapter 49 of the novel has Zhuge Liang build a seven-star altar and summon the south-east wind for Zhou Yu. Nothing of the kind is in the *Sanguozhi*, where the wind simply changed and Huang Gai's fire ships did the work under Zhou Yu's command. Zhuge Liang was not present at Red Cliffs in any command capacity.
- Second example: the "empty city" stratagem (novel chapter 95), in which Zhuge Liang bluffs Sima Yi by sitting on an open city wall, comes from a fragment of the third-century *Wei lüe* 魏略 preserved in commentary, where the deceiver is the Wei commander Wen Ping 文聘 and the deceived is Sun Quan. It had no original connection to Zhuge Liang. [de Crespigny I, fig. 3 caption]
- Third example: Zhuge Liang's southern campaign and the seven captures of Meng Huo 孟获 occupy chapters 87–91 and a whole cycle of traditional dramas.
- **Guan Yu** was a middling general executed in 219. His cult developed slowly: adopted as a monastery protector by Buddhists in the Tang, recruited by Daoist ritual specialists as an exorcist general in the Song. Emperor Huizong of Song brought him into the official Daoist canon. The Ming founder raised him to "emperor"; the Ming government eventually elevated him to thearch as **Guandi** 关帝, and the Qing continued the patronage. By the end of the Qing he was venerated by Confucians as a sage, by Daoists as Holy Ruler Guan, and by Buddhists as Sangharama Bodhisattva, and was often paired with Confucius as the military counterpart 武圣 wǔshèng to the civil sage. Barend ter Haar's *Guan Yu: The Religious Afterlife of a Failed Hero* (Oxford, 2017) is the standard scholarly account. The specific imperial dates circulating online (1594, 1614, 1615) conflict and should not be stated precisely.
- Xiaofei Tian's *The Halberd at Red Cliff: Jian'an and the Three Kingdoms* (Harvard, 2018) argues that both Jian'an and the Three Kingdoms were, from the beginning, ideas constructed in retrospective nostalgia, and that the memory split into a literary (wen 文) and a martial (wu 武) strand. If you name a scholar for the "constructed afterwards" argument the way the Tang card names Naitō Konan, Tian is the one. Her JAOS article on the Shu and Wu perspectives, which I read in full, makes the compatible and directly evidenced argument about canonisation and textual loss.

### Where the period sits in the arc

- The Three Kingdoms opened, rather than interrupted, a long division. The Jin reunification of 280 lasted about a decade in practice: Sima Yan died in 290, the War of the Eight Princes ran 291–306, and the north was lost in the 310s. From 317 the Jin court ruled from Jiankang 建康, the former Wu capital at present-day Nanjing. de Crespigny's point: the refuge was available because Wu had spent sixty years making the south into registered, administered, taxable territory.
- The Xiongnu five divisions that Cao Cao settled inside the frontier, and the vacuum left in the north-east after Sima Yi's Liaodong campaign, are direct antecedents of the Sixteen Kingdoms.
- The nine-rank system carried the great families through Jin and the Southern Dynasties and was only displaced by examination recruitment under Sui and Tang.
- The whole span 220–589 is the corpus's `division` card, "Age of Division".

### Tier 1, new cards the article is incoherent without

- `cao-cao` — Cao Cao 曹操 as a person. The corpus has no card for the most consequential figure of the period, and he is named in four existing card texts. Would cover the parallel government at Xu, the tuntian, the talent edicts, the poetry, and the tomb.
- `zhuge-liang` — Zhuge Liang 诸葛亮 as a person, distinct from the northern expeditions event card. Regent, administrator, and the object of the largest cult of any minister in Chinese history.
- `sanguo-yanyi` — *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* 三国演义. The single biggest gap. The novel is more widely known than the history and shapes every reader's prior. Needs textual history, the Mao recension, and the inversion of Chen Shou.
- `sanguozhi` — *Records of the Three Kingdoms* 三国志 and Pei Songzhi's commentary. The source card for everything on this page.
- `nine-rank-system` — 九品中正制. Runs from 220 to the Sui, is the mechanism by which the aristocracy became hereditary, and is the direct antecedent of the examination system already carded at `sui-examinations`.
- `liu-bei` — Liu Bei 刘备, the founder whose claim drives eight centuries of the legitimacy argument.
- `sun-quan` — Sun Quan 孙权, fifty years in power, and the state that opened the south.

### Tier 2, strongly wanted

- `tuntian` — agricultural garrisons 屯田, the model for state land systems down to the Tang equal-field system
- `sima-yi` — Sima Yi 司马懿 and the 249 coup
- `guan-yu` — Guan Yu 关羽 and his religious afterlife, on ter Haar
- `nanjing` — the city as Jianye, Jiankang, and later capital. Referenced constantly, has no card.
- `great-families` — 门阀, the magnate lineages, their private armies and fortified manors. The Tang card proposes `great-clans` for the same institution; these should probably be one card spanning Han to Tang.
- `zhengtong-debate` — 正统, the legitimate-succession argument, from Chen Shou through Xi Zuochi to Zhu Xi. A concept card that would serve the Song, Yuan and Ming cards too.
- `shanyue` — the Shanyue 山越 and the colonisation of the southeast
- `jian-an-literature` — 建安 poetry, the Cao family, the Seven Masters, the 217 plague
- `xuanxue` — 玄学, pure conversation, Wang Bi, the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove
- `cao-cao-tomb` — the 2009 Xigaoxue excavation and the authenticity dispute. A good archaeology-as-method card.
- `conquest-of-wu-280` — the 280 campaign as an event, since the corpus has Red Cliffs but no card for the reunification itself

### Tier 3, eventually

- `guandu` — the 200 campaign
- `wei-japan-embassies` — the Wa account in Sanguozhi 30, foundational for early Japanese history
- `sichuan` — Sichuan 四川 as a strategic region, invoked by several existing cards with no card of its own
- `celestial-masters` — 天师道, Zhang Lu's Hanzhong state and the origins of organised Daoism
- `hua-tuo` and `zhang-zhongjing` — third-century medicine against the backdrop of the epidemics
- `bielenstein-census` — probably not its own card, but the reliability of Chinese census data deserves a paragraph somewhere, and this period is where it bites hardest
