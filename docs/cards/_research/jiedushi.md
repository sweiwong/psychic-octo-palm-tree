## The argument

The jiedushi was a competent solution to a real problem that was designed with safeguards, and the safeguards were quietly dropped. Between about 710 and the 720s the Tang replaced its slow system of ad hoc expeditionary generalships with permanent frontier commands, because militia levies could not defend a border against Tibetan, Turkic and Khitan raiders who arrived and left before an army could be mustered. The court knew the concentration of power was dangerous, and it built in countermeasures: short tenures of roughly three years, appointment of high civil officials who expected to return to the capital, army supervisors reporting independently, princes given nominal commands. All of these lapsed in the 730s and 740s while the establishment kept growing, until by 742 about 85 per cent of the empire's soldiers stood under nine or ten commissioners and the capital held nothing comparable. The second half of the argument corrects the popular picture of what came after. "Warlord fragmentation" describes north-eastern Hebei, and Zhang Guogang's work shows it does not describe the other forty-odd provinces, most of which were created by the court to police each other, were run by civil bureaucrats where possible, remitted revenue, and became the main route into official service for men the examinations had left out. Emperor Xianzong recovered court control over almost every senior provincial appointment by 820. The structural fault was never fully repaired, and the Song fixed it by abolishing the office in substance and keeping it as an honorific, an over-correction that left the dynasty militarily weak on its own frontiers for three centuries.

## Facts

### Origin and pre-history

- The word **jiedu** (节度) predates the office by six centuries as a temporary warrant, not a post. Earliest recorded use 108 CE, when Liang Dong (梁懂) was made special commissioner for all troops (诸军节度使) of the Later Han western armies. In 263 the commanders who conquered Shu held a jiedu warrant conferred by the regent Sima Zhao (司马昭, 211–265). Li Shimin, later Emperor Taizong, held the title jiedu as supervisor of troops in the Shaandong (陕东) circuit. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi*
- Institutional ancestry: the Six Dynasties area commander with special warrant (使持节都督 shǐ chíjié dūdū); the Northern Zhou area commander-in-chief (总管 zǒngguǎn); the Tang commander-in-chief (都督 dūdū) heading an area command (都督府 dūdūfǔ). Emperor Yang of Sui abolished the zongguan; the Tang revived it as dudu. Area commands were abolished in the interior early and survived mainly in the Western Regions. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi*
- **The problem the office solved.** Tang defence rested on small garrisons (镇戍 zhènshù) of a few hundred men under local civil administration. Anything larger required the central government to raise an expeditionary army (行军 xíngjūn) by mobilising militia units and conscripts, and to appoint a commander and staff for the duration, usually from the capital guards. Everything had to be improvised: mobilisation, training, transport, provisioning. It worked for planned punitive expeditions. It was useless against fast raiders with limited objectives, who had normally withdrawn before the army arrived. *Twitchett, Cambridge History of China vol. 3 ch. 7*
- Under Gaozong and Empress Wu, permanent armies were stationed on the frontier, first in the north-west against Tibet and in Central Asia, and from the 690s along the northern borders of Hedong and Hebei. Even the best of these were too small to withstand a major attack alone. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- **710**: *Zizhi tongjian* 210 records that Xue Ne (薛讷) was appointed Grand General of the Left Militant Guard and concurrently commander of Youzhou, and states that the name jiedushi begins with Xue Ne. *zh.wikipedia, 节度使, citing Zizhi tongjian*
- **711** (Jingyun 2, fourth month): Heba Yansi (贺拔延嗣), of Tuoba descent, was made commander (dudu) of Liangzhou in the function of jiedushi of Hexi. This is the standard date for the office becoming a regular post rather than a temporary function. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi; zh.wikipedia, 节度使*
- Twitchett dates the shift to "beginning in 710–11" and stresses that the system was not set up at once but evolved over the following decade. *Twitchett, CHC3*

### What the office was

- Investiture ritual, from *Xin Tangshu*, Baiguan zhi 4: the commissioner reports to the Ministry of War with his treasury and arms; on taking leave he is granted a double banner and double tally (双旌双节 shuāng jīng shuāng jié); on the march he raises the tally and six standards (六纛 liùdào); prefectures and counties along his route build a reception tower (节楼) and meet him with drums and horns. The office is named after the banner (旌节 jīngjié). *zh.wikipedia, 节度使, quoting Xin Tangshu; Theobald, ChinaKnowledge*
- Each commissioner had a large staff and a large headquarters army, usually called his defence army (经略军 jīnglüèjūn), under direct command, plus full military jurisdiction over a specified number of border prefectures and command of every separate army, garrison and detachment in the zone. Manpower at one governor's disposal ran from 20,000 to more than 90,000. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- Powers accumulated by stages, not by a single grant:
  - **Supply.** Provisioning commissioners (支度使 zhīdùshǐ) handled logistics with large central funds. Military agro-colonies (屯田 túntián) were run by state-lands commissioners (营田使 yíngtiánshǐ). From the early 720s both posts were held concurrently by the jiedushi, with extra specialist staff. *Twitchett, CHC3*
  - **Transport and salt.** After 729 the Fanyang governors also carried responsibility for sea transport; Shuofang governors controlled Yellow River transport linking their armies and local salt production. *Twitchett, CHC3*
  - **Civil authority.** Shuofang governors held the civil inspectorate (采访使 cǎifǎngshǐ) of Guannei in 734 and again 746; Jiannan governors held the civil inspectorate of Western Shannan from 737. From the Tianbao period commissioners were generally given jurisdiction over the local investigation commissioners, unifying military, civil, judicial and fiscal power in one man. *Twitchett, CHC3; Theobald, ChinaKnowledge*
  - **Formal recognition, 725**: commissioners were granted wooden tallies giving them formal authority to undertake major financial transactions and to command large forces on their own authority. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- Bureau staff: vice commissioner (副使 fùshǐ), agent (支使 zhīshǐ), adjutant (行军司马 xíngjūn sīmǎ), administrative assistant (判官 pànguān), judge (推官 tuīguān). Garrison staff controlled by officers (押衙 yāyá), disciplinary inspectors (虞候 yúhòu) and commanders (兵马使 bīngmǎshǐ). *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge*
- The commissioner was appointed by the court; every subordinate was chosen by him and merely approved by the court. Even prefects (刺史 cìshǐ) were filled with men convenient to him. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge*
- Highest honour attached to the post: concurrent appointment as joint manager of affairs with the Secretariat-Chancellery (同中书门下平章事), abbreviated 使相 shǐxiàng, giving a commissioner formal influence on central decisions. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge*
- Because a commissioner controlled several defence commands (镇 zhèn), the term 节镇 jiézhèn was also used; in the late Tang, 藩镇 fānzhèn / 方镇 fāngzhèn ("defence command") displaced 道 dào and even 州 zhōu in ordinary usage, and circuits were renamed after their armies: Zelu (泽潞) circuit was called the Zhaoyi Army (昭义军). *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge*

### The safeguards, and their abandonment

- **Rotation.** Until the mid-730s or later, most commissioners outside the western commands were high civil officials holding concurrent central posts, expecting to be transferred back to the capital. All the first five governors of Fanyang served as chief minister at some point in their careers. They were appointed for short periods, seldom more than four years in one command. Twitchett's judgement: these extremely powerful forces were thus firmly under court control. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- Peterson gives the normal tenure as **three years**, and calls it "quite irregular" that An Lushan was left in place for a dozen years and more. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- **Exceptions from the start.** Anxi, Beiting, Hexi and Longyou, where border war was continuous, were commanded by professional soldiers who served longer, moved between western commands and rarely held civil office. From these emerged a cadre of career frontier officers found in every northern command by the 730s. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- **Supervision.** Governors were accompanied by an army supervisor (监军 jiānjūn), usually a censor, and after 737 sometimes a eunuch. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- **Nominal princes.** In 716 and again in 729 Xuanzong appointed royal princes as nominal jiedushi. The princes stayed at the capital and deputies did the work. Twitchett reads this as a symbolic assertion that commissioners were servants of the throne, not semi-independent satraps. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- **When the personnel changed.** Peterson dates the shift from court-oriented civil officials to professional military officers, many of them non-Chinese, to "from about 730", and explains it as a practical preference for military specialists. He notes in a footnote that Pulleyblank and most other writers instead adopt the traditional explanation, that Li Linfu engineered the change to protect his own position at court. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8, and n. 5* **See Contested §3, because `an-lushan.md` states the Li Linfu explanation as fact.**
- O'Byrne calculates that **21 per cent** of the men who served as military governors between 755 and 779 had risen from the ranks; most of those would have begun their careers before 755. *cited in Graff, "The Reach of the Military: Tang"*

### The military system underneath

- Graff's three phases of Tang military institutions: (1) roughly sixty years from the 620s, the militia (府兵 fǔbīng) as backbone; (2) 680s to 755, transitional, militia superseded by long-service professionals in permanent frontier armies; (3) 755 to beyond 907, full-time professionals in provincial garrisons. *Graff, "The Reach of the Military: Tang"*
- The militia: several hundred regimental headquarters (折冲府 zhéchōngfǔ after 636) of 600 to 1,000 men each, affiliated to capital guard commands, doing one-month tours by rotation. Men enrolled about age twenty and served to sixty; in exchange for tax exemption they furnished their own provisions and most equipment. At its late-seventh-century height perhaps 600,000 men. *Graff, same*
- **12 June 737 edict**: commissioners were to calculate manpower needs and set quotas filled by voluntary long-service enlistment from serving conscripts and migrant households (客户 kèhù), with above-standard pay, permanent tax and corvée exemption, and land and houses for dependents who came to the frontier. Long-service men were called 健儿 jiàn'ér. *Graff, same; Twitchett, CHC3*
- **First month of 738**: quotas near filled, conscription for frontier service abolished, remaining conscripts sent home. *Graff, same; Twitchett, CHC3*
- **Also 737**: commissioners were ordered to agree a fixed establishment with the Secretariat-Chancellery. The resulting establishment survives in a form dating from about 742. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- **749**: Li Linfu issued an edict stopping the militia regiments from sending troops to the capital. The edict says they had recently been nominal establishments without organisation, officers, arms, equipment or provisions. Twitchett's framing: the abandonment of the idea, current since the Northern Wei, that an effective army could be kept cheaply by training self-supporting farmers. *Twitchett, CHC3* **This is the more precise version of `an-lushan.md`'s "abolished in 749".**
- Part-time local militia (团结兵 tuánjiébīng, later 团练兵) continued, first raised in Hebei against the Khitan in the 690s, under the local prefect, defensive only. About 14,000 around Chengdu. *Twitchett, CHC3; Graff, same*

### The scale by 742

- Total imperial establishment 574,733 men, slightly more than 1 per cent of the population; frontier troops about 490,000 with more than 80,000 cavalry horses. **No less than 85 per cent of the empire's troops, and almost all its battle-experienced soldiers, were under the commissioners, with no comparable force at the central government's disposal.** Twitchett notes this is why Li Linfu and Niu Xianke clung to their own nominal commands. *Twitchett, CHC3*
- Graff gives frontier armies as **476,900** for 742 (Jiu Tangshu 9, Zizhi tongjian 215) and a registered population of 48,909,800, a military participation ratio of 1:103, comparable to the late Roman empire and lighter than France under Louis XIV (1:50) or Prussia in 1740 (1:27). *Graff, same*
- ChinaKnowledge, from the Jiu Tangshu 38 table, gives the ten commands as: Fanyang 91,400; Longyou 75,000; Hexi 73,000; Shuofang 64,700; Hedong 55,000; Pinglu 37,500; Jiannan 30,900; Anxi 24,000; Beiting 20,000; the five Lingnan prefectures 15,400. Total 486,900. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- **Nine of the ten were jiedushi; the tenth, Lingnan, was a regulatory commissioner (经略使 jīnglüèshǐ).** Both Graff and Theobald say so explicitly. Most popular accounts say "ten jiedushi". *Graff, same; Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- Peterson: military power was concentrated in five commands along the northern frontier, in order of size Fanyang, Longyou, Hexi, Shuofang, Hedong. Fanyang, and to a lesser extent Hedong, were largely supplied locally and needed less from the centre than the others. Fanyang with its subordinate Pinglu held about 130,000 men by about 742. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- Pinglu, though a separate command, was under Fanyang's general control until 742. *Twitchett, CHC3, table 8 note*
- **Cost.** Frontier defence costs rose five-fold between 714 and 741, and a further 40 to 50 per cent between 742 and 755. Li Jinxiu's analysis of Du You's Tianbao figures puts military spending at 9,042,500 strings of cash out of total annual expenditure of 33,064,300, or 27.35 per cent. *Twitchett, CHC3; Li Jinxiu via Graff, same*

### After 763: the shape of provincial government

- **Number of provinces.** Peterson: the settlement at the close of the rebellion established some **thirty-four** provincial units; the number grew, later varying between **forty-five and fifty**. Virtually all of the thirty in existence in the first post-war years survived; by 785 all provinces of any significance existed. Graff: from ten frontier commands to **more than forty**, most now in the interior. Theobald: "more than twenty regions controlled by military commissioners" in the second half of the Tang. Zhang Guogang: close to fifty, stable at forty or fifty. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8; Graff, same; Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi; Zhang Guogang via Sohu and Guangming*
- **Why the court multiplied them.** Most of the new commands were created during the emergency and given to loyalist commanders as bases from which to extract men, material and money; the deliberate design was that one commissioner could check another. *Graff, same; Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- Peterson's qualification: the breakdown of the old centralised institutions genuinely required more devolution than before, and some new provinces were created purely for administrative convenience. Hunan and Eyue were carved out of the oversized Jingnan and Jiangxi in 764 and 765 because the existing arrangements were inadequate, not to weaken anyone. "The Tang regime survived only at the cost of becoming highly decentralized." *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- **Not all provinces had a jiedushi.** In less critical places the head was a defence commissioner (防御使 fángyùshǐ) or a military training commissioner (团练使 tuánliànshǐ), and in the south a civil governor. Almost all northern provinces were headed by jiedushi, who invariably also held the civil governorship (观察使 guāncháshǐ, successor to Xuanzong's civil inspectors, now with wide administrative powers). *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8; Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- Provincial armies ranged from a few thousand to 75,000–100,000 men. Provinces ranged from two prefectures to a dozen and more. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- Total men under arms at the end of the rebellion, including frontier forces, about **850,000**, excluding disbanded militia. "Yet in such a highly militarized state there was not one central army of any size at the disposition of the court." *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- By 805 there were roughly 800,000 regulars; by Muzong's reign nearly a million. *Hino via Graff, same*
- **Provincial army structure.** The main concentration was the headquarters garrison (牙军 yájūn) at the provincial seat, normally the only force with real striking power, with an inner elite guard (牙内军 yánèijūn) of private soldiers paid from the governor's purse, fictive adopted sons and retainers who followed him between posts. Outer garrisons (外镇 wàizhèn) held prefectural and county seats, trade centres and salt pools. Elite guard officers repeatedly attempted or achieved usurpation. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- **The soldiers as an interest group.** Late Tang regulars (官健 guānjiàn) were full-time mercenaries, completely separated from civilian life, paid in grain, cloth and allowances of salt, wine, soy sauce and vinegar, plus campaign bonuses, festival bonuses and an installation bonus for each new governor. Many units became hereditary. Zhang Guogang counts **ninety-nine mutinies** in late Tang provincial armies, with money the single largest cause. In the tenth month of 783 Dezong was driven from Chang'an by the Jingyuan (泾原) troops, who found their departure bonus of silk smaller than expected. *Graff, same, citing Zhang Guogang, Tangdai fanzhen yanjiu*
- **The fiscal split.** Revenue was nominally divided three ways: a share sent to the capital, a share retained by the province, a share by the prefectures. The provincial share was usually the largest and was at the commissioner's disposal. Many refused to forward the capital's share. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi*
- The financial report of **807**, the *Yuanhe guojibu* (元和国计簿), attests effective central control over finances in only **eight provinces of the Yangtze valley**, and registration of taxpaying households down at least 30 per cent from the first year of Dezong's reign. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- Standard provincial dodges: keeping a second household list unreported to the centre; the 影庇 practice, in which wealthy landowners nominally assigned land to tax-exempt officials and paid for the shelter; illegal supplementary levies; internal customs on trade. Han Hong (韩弘) at Xuanwu, astride the Bian Canal, retired in 819 with a fortune in the millions. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- **The career ladder.** The commissioner had virtually full appointing power over his own staff (使府 shǐfǔ) and officers, a route into office entirely separate from the Board of Civil Office. The pool it drew on was wider: literati who had not passed the examinations or had passed but not obtained a post (Han Yu 韩愈 among them), soldiers turned administrator, men of low origin with enough literacy. Peterson: "Advancement under these provincial authorities became, in fact, the most important avenue of social mobility during the second half of the dynasty." Such men were technically "outside the current" (流外 liúwài) and, apart from military officers, almost never crossed into central government service. Bai Juyi observed that provincial officials were more numerous and better paid than regular local officials. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- Zhang Guogang's related figure: roughly **two-thirds** of Tang chancellors had at some point served on a provincial headquarters staff (幕府 mùfǔ). *Zhang Guogang via Sohu* **Treat as his figure and attribute; see Contested §7.**

### Zhang Guogang's four types

Zhang Guogang (张国刚 Zhāng Guógāng), *Tangdai fanzhen yanjiu* (唐代藩镇研究, Changsha, 1987), classifies post-755 provinces into four kinds and gives disturbance counts out of 171 recorded provincial disturbances:

| Type | Examples | Character | Share of the 171 disturbances |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separatist, Hebei (河朔割据型) | the three Hebei garrisons, Yiding, Cangjing, Huaixi, Ziqing | manned by An Lushan's former officers; commissioners installed themselves, forwarded no tax | 65, about 38% |
| Central Plains containment (中原防遏型) | Xuanwu, Wuning, Zhongwu, Zelu, Heyang, Yicheng | large standing forces created to box in the Hebei group and protect the southeastern grain route | 52, about 30% |
| Frontier defence (边疆御边型) | eight in the north-west, nine in the south-west | 200,000-plus men, dependent on central supply; 29 of 42 disturbances caused by shortfalls in pay and rations | 42 |
| South-eastern revenue (东南财源型) | Zhedong, Zhexi, Huainan, Jingnan and five others | weak forces, rich economies, the fiscal base of the court | 12, under 7% |

Zhang's headline claim: treating 藩镇割据 as an inseparable phrase misleads, because genuine separatism describes only the first category, and after Xianzong only the three Hebei garrisons; the rest propped the dynasty up. He also argues the court retained personnel powers even in Hebei. *Zhang Guogang via Sohu and Guangming Daily*

### The recovery under Xianzong

- Emperor Xianzong (宪宗, r. 805–820). Peterson treats his reign as the second formative period in centre-province relations after 755, and says the structure it produced lasted with little modification until the Tang collapse in the third quarter of the ninth century. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- Sequence: Liu Bi at Jiannan-Xichuan and Yang Huilin at Xiasui suppressed in 806; Li Qi at Zhenhai in 807; a failed campaign against Wang Chengzong of Chengde under the eunuch commander Tutu Chengcui in 809–810, costing 7,000,000 strings of cash; the Huaixi war against Wu Yuanji from 814, in which the chancellor Wu Yuanheng was assassinated in 815 and Pei Du (裴度, 765–839, a jinshi of 789) was given overall command; Li Su captured Wu Yuanji in 817; the Pinglu/Ziqing campaign launched late 818, Li Shidao assassinated by his own men early 819. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen; Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8; Graff, same*
- After Ziqing fell the court broke it into three provinces (a reduced Pinglu of five prefectures, Tianping of three, Yanhai of four), and they sent nothing to the capital until 832. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- **Spring 819 reform**, the most significant of Xianzong's measures: commissioners lost authority over all military units outside their home prefecture, and those units passed to the prefect in whose prefecture they stood. Prefects were given permanent explicit military powers for the first time since the Six Dynasties. Border regions were exempted. The model was a memorial by Wu Chongyin, newly appointed governor of Henghai, who argued that Hebei had defied the court for sixty years because garrison commanders had displaced prefects and magistrates in every prefecture and county. Commissioners kept their headquarters garrisons, which were their best troops. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- A parallel 809 measure attacked provincial finances; later measures ended agricultural colonies as a provincial revenue source and stopped governors appointing county magistrates. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- **By 820 the court had consolidated control over the appointment and installation of the highest provincial officials everywhere except the three Hebei provinces (Weibo, Chengde, Youzhou), and even two of those had conceded the court's right to name successors.** Han Hong, the last long-entrenched satrap outside Hebei, gave up Xuanwu voluntarily and moved to the capital. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- Peterson's verdict: measured against the preceding reigns rather than against total recentralisation, Xianzong "emerges as the only really successful emperor of the second half of the dynasty", and his changes "sharply reduced the possibility of any military threat from the provinces". *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*
- The gains partly unravelled under Muzong (穆宗, r. 820–824), who cut military spending and dismissed troops. Lulong's officers killed the incoming commissioner's staff; Chengde's officers killed Tian Hongzheng. Discharged troops went to Hebei and strengthened it. Pei Du's punitive campaign failed and the court accepted a long-term autonomous north-east. Zelu became a hereditary line under Liu Wu, his son Liu Congjian and grandson Liu Zhen, ended by the Huichang campaign of 844. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*

### The end of the Tang and the Five Dynasties

- After Huang Chao took Chang'an in 881 the central government effectively ceased to exist. Commissioners acted independently, and men who had been military leaders first and were then confirmed as commissioners declared themselves rulers: Yang Xingmi (杨行密, 852–905, Wu), Dong Chang (董昌, 847–896, Yue), Qian Liu (钱镠, 852–932, Wu-Yue). *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- **907**: Zhu Wen (朱温, 852–912), simultaneously commissioner of Xuanwu, Xuanyi, Tianping, Huguo and Zhongwu, deposed the last Tang emperor and founded the Later Liang. Southern commissioners then founded their own states. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- **In the Five Dynasties the office reached its peak.** The founding emperor of every one of the five northern dynasties was a jiedushi: Zhu Wen (Later Liang), Li Cunxu (李存勖, Later Tang), Shi Jingtang (石敬瑭, Later Jin), Liu Zhiyuan (刘知远, Later Han), Guo Wei (郭威, Later Zhou). Commissioners made and unmade emperors. *zh.wikipedia, 节度使*
- A distinction worth keeping: Zhu Wen, Li Cunxu, Shi Jingtang and Liu Zhiyuan were territorial commissioners who took the throne from their provinces. Guo Wei and Zhao Kuangyin were commanders of the metropolitan guard who also held a commissionership. *zh.wikipedia, 节度使*
- Wang Gungwu's *The Structure of Power in North China during the Five Dynasties* (SOAS PhD 1957, University of Malaya Press 1963) treats the period as dominated by the military governors together with the administrators and officers of their provinces. *SOAS review record; publisher listings*
- **The dynasty name.** Zhao Kuangyin (赵匡胤) held two posts at once in 959–960: commander of the palace guard (殿前都点检 diànqián dū diǎnjiǎn) and commissioner of the Guide Army (归德军 Guīdéjūn), seated at Songzhou (宋州, modern Shangqiu, Henan). When he took the throne in 960 the dynasty was named Song after that command. *Baidu Baike, 陈桥兵变; standard accounts*

### The Song extinction of the office

- Zhao Pu's (赵普, 922–992) recorded prescription, in reply to Taizu's question about why eight ruling houses had come and gone since the Tang: reduce their authority, control their money and grain, take away their best troops (稍夺其权，制其钱谷，收其精兵). *zh.wikipedia, 杯酒释兵权*
- **961**, ninth day of the seventh month of Jianlong 2: the banquet of the "cup of wine" (杯酒释兵权 bēijiǔ shì bīngquán), with Shi Shouxin (石守信), Gao Huaide (高怀德), Wang Shenqi (王审琦), Zhang Lingduo (张令铎), Zhao Yanhui (赵彦徽) and Luo Yangui (罗彦瓌). The next day they resigned on grounds of illness and were granted generous retirements. **Note what actually happened to them: they gave up metropolitan guard commands and were moved into jiedushi posts in the provinces.** Shi Shouxin went from metropolitan guard commander to commissioner of Tianping, keeping the guard title as an empty honour. *zh.wikipedia, 杯酒释兵权, citing Songshi 250*
- **969**, tenth month of Kaibao 2: a second banquet, this one with actual provincial commissioners, named as Wang Yanchao (王彦超), Guo Congyi (郭从义), Wu Xingde (武行德), Bai Zhongzan (白重赞) and Yang Tingzhang (杨廷璋). They were stripped of command and given high honorary titles; Wang Yanchao became a Guard general and Duke of Bin. **This, not 961, is the episode about the jiedushi.** *Chinese-language accounts summarising Xu Zizhi tongjian changbian; zh.wikipedia, 杯酒释兵权*
- Taizu's structural measures: strip commissioners of financial power, leaving only what was needed to pay their troops; **965**, order that the best troops from every command be sent to garrison the capital; subordinate all local troops in central China to central command and appoint no commissioners there; send civil officials from the capital to head prefectures. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi*
- The permanent machinery: prefects (知州 zhīzhōu) rotated every three years and shadowed by controllers-general (通判 tōngpàn) entitled to report to the capital without the prefect's knowledge; prefectural revenue forwarded straight to the capital by transport commissioners (转运使 zhuǎnyùnshǐ); capital punishment reserved to the centre; elite soldiers garrisoned around the capital as the standard (兵样 bīngyàng) for provincial units; the imperial army (禁军 jìnjūn) split into three divisions (三衙 sānyá) with command authority but no power to deploy, which lay with the Bureau of Military Affairs (枢密院 shūmìyuàn); generals rotated to stop them building ties to their troops; about half the army stationed around the capital. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, Political System of the Song Empire*
- **977** (Taiping Xingguo 2), under Taizong: commissioners lost their branch prefectures (支郡 zhījùn), which reported directly to the centre, leaving each with one prefecture at most. *zh.wikipedia and Chinese reference summaries of the Taiping Xingguo edicts.* **ChinaKnowledge attributes this measure to Taizu, who died in November 976. See Contested §8.**
- What the title became: the top rank for military officers, and an honorific for imperial clansmen, empresses' kin, southwestern tribal chieftains under the 羁縻 jīmí system, and the sons of meritorious generals, all with full ceremonial. Song holders were granted three seals and an elaborate set of banners, standards and a leopard-tail pennant. Before Zhezong the title was not lightly given; under Huizong six eunuchs received it. In the Southern Song men could hold three nominal commissionerships at once with no troops attached, Yue Fei and Han Shizhong among them. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi; zh.wikipedia, 节度使*
- Liao and Jin copied the office, largely nominal. The Yuan abolished it. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, jiedushi; zh.wikipedia, 节度使*

### The long tail: a Tang command becomes a dynasty

- The Xiazhou command (夏州节度使) was created out of Shuofang territory during Dezong's Zhenyuan period (785–805), seated at Xiazhou (modern Baichengzi, Jingbian county, Shaanxi), one of the eight commands west of the capital.
- **881**: the Tangut leader Tuoba Sigong (拓跋思恭) was appointed there for fighting Huang Chao, granted the imperial surname Li and the title Duke of Xia. **882**: the command was renamed the Dingnan Army (定难军节度使 Dìngnánjūn jiédùshǐ).
- The Li family held it without a break through the Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, Later Zhou and into the Song. Li Jiqian (李继迁) refused to submit in 982 and took Liao recognition. Li Deming and then Li Yuanhao held the post to 1038, when Yuanhao declared the Western Xia empire. **A Tang frontier commissionership passed through nine dynasties and became a state 157 years later.** *zh.wikipedia, 定难军节度使, sourced to Xin Tangshu, Fangzhen biao*

### Sources of evidence, worth naming

- *Xin Tangshu* (新唐书) devotes chapter 64 to a chronological table of defence commands (方镇表 Fāngzhèn biǎo) and chapters 210–214 to biographies of hereditary commissioners (藩镇传 Fānzhèn zhuàn). The dynastic history treats them as a category of its own. *Theobald, ChinaKnowledge, fanzhen*
- Li Jifu's geographical survey of 814, the *Yuanhe junxian tuzhi* (元和郡县图志), organises the empire by these provinces as facts of life. *Peterson, CHC3 ch. 8*

## Contested

Each with the nature of the dispute and suggested caveat phrasing. The style guide requires at least one; use §1 or §3 or §9, and ideally two.

1. **When the office begins: 710 or 711.** *Zizhi tongjian* 210 says the name jiedushi begins with Xue Ne in 710. Heba Yansi's appointment to Hexi in the fourth month of 711 is the standard date for the office becoming a regular post. Both are correct claims about different things. Twitchett sidesteps by writing "beginning in 710–11". *Suggested phrasing: the title is first recorded in 710 and became a standing post the following year.*

2. **The founding date of every individual command is unreliable.** Twitchett's own table gives Pinglu 719, Fanyang 714, Hedong before 723, Shuofang 713 or 721, Longyou 714, Hexi 711, Jiannan 717 or 719, Beiting 727 or 733, Anxi 718, with a footnote that "the dates given for the foundation of the different commands vary widely, and it is often difficult to decide whether a given date refers to the title of military governor, or to the formal establishment of the command organization." Chinese-language reference tables give a materially different set (Anxi 710, Beiting 712, Hexi 710, Hedong 711, Fanyang 713, Longyou 713, Jiannan 714, Pinglu 719, Shuofang 721, Lingnan 711). *Recommendation: do not give per-command founding dates at all. Say the system was assembled between about 710 and the early 720s.*

3. **Why professional soldiers replaced civil officials in the commands.** The traditional account, followed by Pulleyblank and repeated in most reference works and in `an-lushan.md`, is that Li Linfu deliberately filled the frontier commands with non-Chinese generals from about 747 to stop rivals using a military reputation to reach the chancellorship. Peterson dates the change earlier, to about 730, and attributes it to a practical preference for military specialists, relegating the Li Linfu explanation to a footnote as the view of others. *Suggested phrasing: the shift to career soldiers is usually credited to the chancellor Li Linfu protecting his own position; Peterson dates it earlier and reads it as a straightforward preference for professionals.* **Do not contradict `an-lushan.md` outright; add the qualification.**

4. **The number of provinces after 763.** Peterson: about thirty-four at the settlement, later forty-five to fifty. Graff: ten becoming more than forty. Zhang Guogang: close to fifty, stable at forty or fifty. Theobald: more than twenty *controlled by military commissioners*, which is a narrower claim, because not every province had a jiedushi. *Suggested phrasing: forty to fifty provinces, of which only some were headed by a military commissioner.*

5. **The 742 frontier troop total.** 486,900 from the Jiu Tangshu 38 table; 476,900 in Graff from Jiu Tangshu 9 and Zizhi tongjian 215; about 490,000 in Twitchett; about 489,400 in some tallies. `an-lushan.md` uses 486,900. *Recommendation: use 486,900 for consistency with the sibling card, or round to "close to half a million".*

6. **"Ten jiedushi".** Nine of the ten Tianbao commands were headed by a jiedushi; Lingnan was headed by a jinglüeshi. The shorthand "ten military commissioners of Tianbao" (天宝十节度使) is the Chinese term of art and is what `an-lushan.md` uses. *Suggested phrasing: ten standing frontier commands, nine of them under a military commissioner.*

7. **Zhang Guogang's statistics.** The 171 disturbances, the four-way percentage split and the "two-thirds of chancellors served in a provincial headquarters" figure come from Zhang Guogang's own writing and popular summaries of it, not from an independent recount. Graff cites Zhang's mutiny count of ninety-nine directly. *Recommendation: attribute in the text ("Zhang Guogang counts…") rather than stating as neutral fact.*

8. **Which Song emperor stripped the branch prefectures.** ChinaKnowledge says Taizu "in 977 applied this measure for the whole empire". Taizu died in November 976; 977 is Taiping Xingguo 2, the second year of Taizong. Chinese sources put the 罢领支郡 edict under Taizong. *Recommendation: attribute the 977 measure to Taizong, or write "the Song court" and give the year.*

9. **The "cup of wine" anecdote.** Not in the *Veritable Records of Taizu* (太祖实录). The earliest account is in Wang Zeng's (王曾) *Wang Wenzheng gong bilu* (王文正公笔录); Sima Guang's *Sushui jiwen* (涑水记闻) and Wang Gong's *Wenjian jinlu* (闻见近录) also carry it, and the accounts contradict each other on the sequence of events. Li Tao (李焘) reproduced Sima Guang's version in the *Xu Zizhi tongjian changbian* and dated it to the jiawu day of the sixth month of Jianlong 2, which conflicts with the ninth day of the seventh month given elsewhere. Ding Zeliang (丁则良) in 1945 called it hearsay and not credible; Deng Guangming (邓广铭) said there was no evidence for it; Xu Gui (徐规) and Fang Jianxin (方建新) denied the event in 1982 and again in 1995, pointing out that Empress Dowager Du died in the sixth month of 961 so Taizu could not have held a banquet during court mourning, and that veteran generals including Liu Yanrang, Han Chongyun, Li Jixun and Murong Yanzhao continued to command the imperial guard after the alleged date. Liu Liyan (柳立言, 1990) and Wang Yuji (王育济, 1996) defend it. What is not in dispute is that Taizu carried out a concentrated reshuffle of the guard command. *Suggested phrasing: the banquet is a story from Song private notebooks, absent from the official record and doubted since the 1940s; the reshuffle it describes did happen.* **And the harder point the card should make: the 961 banquet, if it happened, moved guard commanders into provincial commissionerships. The banquet that was about the jiedushi is the second one, in 969.**

10. **Who appointed Tuoba Sigong in 881.** Some summaries name Emperor Xianzong, who died in 820. The reigning emperor during Huang Chao's occupation of Chang'an was Xizong (僖宗, r. 873–888). *Use Xizong, or write "the Tang court".*

11. **Reading of 李焘.** Standard romanisation for the Song historian is Lǐ Tāo; the character also reads dào. *Use Lǐ Tāo.*

12. **Reading of 吐蕃.** Mainland dictionaries give Tǔbō; Tǔfān is also current. `an-lushan.md` uses Tǔbō. Match it.

## Image

**Recommended: the Zhang Yichao procession mural, Mogao Cave 156.** It is the only surviving contemporary picture of a Tang military commissioner in the exercise of his office, and it was commissioned by his own family, which makes it evidence about how commissioners wanted to be seen.

- Object: *Zhang Yichao Leading His Army Out* (张议潮统军出行图), lower south wall of the main chamber, Mogao Cave 156 (莫高窟第156窟), Dunhuang. Late Tang, generally dated to about 865 and associated with his nephew and successor Zhang Huaishen. Roughly 108 cm high by 855 cm long; sources also give the length as 8.2 m. Around 238 figures and more than 110 horses: Zhang on a white horse at the centre, banners, drummers, cavalry, musicians, dancers and baggage train.
- Context: Zhang Yichao (799–872) led a rising at Shazhou in 848 that threw the Tibetans out of Dunhuang, sent envoys to Chang'an in 851, and was appointed commissioner of the newly created Guiyi Army and surveillance commissioner of eleven prefectures. His case is the late-Tang pattern in miniature: the court conferred the title on power that already existed.
- Best Commons file checked this session: `File:Dunhuang Zhang Yichao army.jpg`, 1234 × 624 px, https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cf/Dunhuang_Zhang_Yichao_army.jpg, described as a section of the mural, credited "Dunhuang Mogao Caves", public domain (ninth-century wall painting). A detail crop exists as `File:Dunhuang General Zhang Yichao army detail.jpg`, 544 × 729, credited as scanned from *Cave Temples of Dunhuang: Art and History on the Silk Road*.
- **Caveat for the image selector**: all Commons files of this mural have thin provenance metadata (anonymous uploads, no photographer, no institution). The underlying painting is unambiguously public domain, but the photographic reproduction chain is not documented. Check `data/image-coverage.md` before committing, and caption it as a ninth-century wall painting at Mogao Cave 156 photographed at an unknown date.
- **Alternative if that fails**: a Tang glazed figure of an armoured officer from a museum open-access collection, captioned as a tomb figure rather than a portrait. Weaker, because it says nothing specific about the office.
- **Map suggestion, if one is ever commissioned**: two panels side by side, the ten frontier commands of about 742 and the forty-odd provinces of about 785, labelled as modern reconstructions with approximate boundaries. The visual point is the office moving from the rim to the whole surface.
