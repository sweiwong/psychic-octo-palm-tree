# Jiedushi · 节度使 · jiédùshǐ

**710–979**

Jiedushi (节度使 jiédùshǐ) were regional military governors in China during the [[tang|Tang Dynasty]] (618–907 CE) and the subsequent [[five-dynasties|Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period]] (907–960 CE). The term translates literally to "commissioner of a military command" or "military governor." Initially created as a pragmatic response to frontier threats, the jiedushi evolved from loyal instruments of the central court into autonomous warlords whose power rivalled that of the emperor, ultimately contributing to the empire's collapse.

## Origins and Expansion of Power

The office of the jiedushi was established in the early 8th century during the reign of Emperor Xuanzong (r. 712–756) as a solution to mounting military pressure on the empire's borders. By this time, the old fubing militia system (府兵制 fǔbīngzhì), a part-time farmer-soldier levy that had underpinned early Tang military success, had decayed due to land inequality and long campaigns. In its place, the court relied on permanent, professional armies stationed along the northern and western frontiers to defend against threats such as the Tibetan Empire, the Turks, and the Khitan.

To speed up defence, the Tang court consolidated civil and military authority in these frontier zones. A single jiedushi was given command over several prefectures, allowing him to muster troops, requisition supplies, and react quickly to incursions without waiting for imperial approval. By the 740s, ten major commands were established, most notably under non-Han generals like An Lushan, who commanded the northeastern frontier (modern Hebei and Liaoning). Initially, these governors were appointed by the throne, rotated frequently, and remained firmly subordinate to the central government.

## The An Lushan Rebellion and the Turning Point

The catastrophic [[an-lushan|An Lushan Rebellion]] (755–763 CE) marked the definitive shift in the balance of power. An Lushan, a jiedushi of Turkic-Sogdian descent who commanded three large frontier armies, launched a massive revolt against the Tang in 755. He captured the eastern capital, Luoyang, and declared himself emperor of a new dynasty. Although the rebellion was eventually suppressed, largely with the help of other jiedushi and foreign mercenaries, the Tang state was shattered.

To end the crisis, the weakened Tang court granted broad autonomy to military governors across the empire, not just on the frontiers. This system, known as the fanzhen (藩镇 fānzhèn, "buffer garrisons"), effectively institutionalised warlordism. In regions like Hebei, the so-called "Three Garrisons" (Youzhou, Chengde, and Weibo) were ruled by former rebel generals who acknowledged Tang suzerainty in name only. The court, drained of revenue and military strength, was too weak to rein them in. From 763 onward, the jiedushi transitioned from being imperial officials to semi-sovereign rulers.

## Powers and Autonomy

The power of a late-Tang jiedushi extended far beyond simple military command. Over time, they usurped the functions of the civil bureaucracy to become a "state within a state."

*   **Military Command:** They maintained large, personal armies of professional soldiers, often bound to the governor by personal loyalty and financial reward rather than duty to the dynasty. These armies were used to defend territory but also to intimidate the court and suppress local populations.
*   **Tax Collection:** Autonomous jiedushi stopped forwarding tax revenues to the central treasury in Chang'an. They levied their own taxes to fund their armies and courts, stripping the Tang government of the financial resources needed to project power.
*   **Civil Administration:** The governors appointed their own prefects, magistrates, and staff, bypassing the imperial examination system and the Ministry of Personnel. The will of the jiedushi replaced imperial law within their domains.
*   **Hereditary Succession:** Perhaps the most egregious violation of imperial authority was the practice of hereditary succession. In many fanzhen, particularly in the northeast, a jiedushi would designate his son or a trusted subordinate as his successor. The Tang court was usually forced to rubber-stamp the appointment after the fact, acknowledging a reality it could not change.

## The Tang Collapse and the Five Dynasties

During the 9th century, the Tang court made periodic attempts to reassert control, such as under Emperor Xianzong (r. 805–820), who briefly subdued several recalcitrant garrisons. However, these successes were temporary. The empire was further destabilised by the [[huang-chao|Huang Chao Rebellion]] (874–884), a massive peasant uprising that ravaged the south and sacked Chang'an.

In suppressing the rebellion, the court delegated even more power to ambitious jiedushi. Among them was Zhu Wen, a former rebel turned Tang general, who was granted command of the strategic region around the capital. In 904, Zhu Wen assassinated Emperor Zhaozong, and in 907 he [[tang-end|deposed the last Tang emperor, Ai]], declaring himself the first emperor of the [[catalog-R_FIVE_LATER_LIANG|Later Liang dynasty]]. This act formally ended the Tang Dynasty and inaugurated the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period, a chaotic era in which jiedushi carved China into competing states, with the strongest proclaiming themselves emperors over the Central Plains.

## Legacy and the Song Reaction

The collapse of the Tang under the weight of the fanzhen system left a profound impression on the founders of the subsequent [[song|Song Dynasty]] (960–1279). Emperor Taizu of Song, himself a jiedushi who seized the throne via a military coup, moved swiftly to dismantle the system that had destroyed the Tang.

The Song Dynasty abolished the real power of the jiedushi, reducing the title to a purely honorary one. Military authority was centralised under the emperor, with the army reorganised and commanded by rotating civilian officials from the capital. The policy of "emphasising the civil over the military" (重文轻武 zhòng wén qīng wǔ) became a cornerstone of Song governance. While this successfully prevented the re-emergence of autonomous warlords, it also came at a cost: the Song military was often less effective against external threats, such as the [[liao|Khitan Liao]] and [[jurchen-jin|Jurchen Jin]] dynasties.

The jiedushi are one of history's clearest examples of institutional devolution. Created as a tool of imperial defence, they became the architects of imperial fragmentation. Their rise and fall framed the trajectory of the Tang Dynasty and directly shaped the political culture of China for centuries to come.
