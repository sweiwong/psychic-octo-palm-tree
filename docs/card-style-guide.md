# Card style guide: Chinese History Atlas

## What this is

The Chinese History Atlas is a desktop web app covering China from c. 2070 BCE to the present. The home screen is a wrapped multi-row timeline. Clicking any item opens a **card** in a 340px right-hand detail panel. This skill writes the text content of those cards.

Live site: https://china-history-atlas-six.vercel.app/

There are roughly 207 cards in the dataset. Most were originally generated by AI and are weak: they read as stitched-together Wikipedia summaries with inline citations, no argument, and no sense of why anything matters. They are being rewritten one at a time. `docs/cards/tang.md` is the finished reference example. **Read it before writing anything.**

Before writing, read the existing card for the topic in `china_history_expanded.json` so you know its `id`, `category`, `related` array, existing `sources` and existing `image`. Do not invent a new slug for a card that already exists.

Card ids are kebab-case slugs (`an-lushan`, `wu-zhou`, `li-bai-du-fu`). Older imported records carry a `catalog-` prefix with the original uppercase id (`catalog-E_QING_OPIUM`). Match whatever already exists.

## Process

1. **Resolve the topic.** Find the existing card id, or choose a new kebab-case slug if genuinely new. Confirm the category: `dynasty`, `period`, `event`, `culture`, `world`.
2. **Research first.** Web search and read before writing a word of prose. Do not write from memory alone. Prefer museum and university sources, Cambridge History volumes, and specialist scholarship over general reference. Collect dates, figures, named people and institutional detail.
3. **Write the card** to the rules below.
4. **Build the link inventory**: which wikilink targets already exist in the corpus and which need new cards, tiered by priority.
5. **Select an image** to the standards below.
6. **Save** to `docs/cards/<slug>.md` with YAML frontmatter, then tell Wei the file path and the count of new cards the article generated.

## Audience and purpose

Written for a bright 18-year-old: a high schooler or first-year undergraduate, or an adult reading alongside their kids. Intelligent, not dumbed down. The purpose is to make the reader want to go further into Chinese history, so every card should leave doors open to other cards.

## Voice

**The register is factual reference prose written by a historian.** Dense with fact, carrying an argument, readable. Think a very good museum wall text or the opening of a Cambridge History chapter.

**It is not storytelling.** This was tried and explicitly rejected. Do not write scene-setting openings, second-person address ("walk through the gate"), dramatic one-line paragraphs, rhetorical questions, or narrative suspense ("Then it broke"). No hooks. Open with what the thing is.

Wei's writing rules are non-negotiable and apply to every line:

- **No em dashes.** Use commas, periods, colons or parentheses.
- **No negative parallelism.** Never "not X, but Y", "it wasn't X, it was Y". State the point directly.
- **No superficial -ing tails.** Do not append ", highlighting the broader shift" or ", reflecting its importance" to fake depth. Make it a sentence or cut it.
- **No copula avoidance.** Use "is", "are", "has". Never "serves as", "stands as", "represents", "boasts", "features".
- **No chatbot artifacts**, no promotional adjectives, no rule-of-three flourishes.
- British spelling ("militarised", "neighbouring") to match the existing corpus.

Cards also get a no-ai-slop pass, adapted from the `no-ai-slop` skill's detection rules (not its "preserve personal voice" editing principles, which do not apply to impersonal reference prose):

- **No importance puffery.** "Stands as a testament", "marks a pivotal moment", "plays a vital role", "underscores its significance". State the fact and let the reader judge whether it matters.
- **No weasel attribution.** "Historians agree", "many scholars believe", "it is widely held". Name the historian, the way this project already names Naitō Konan for the Tang-Song transition, or cut the claim.
- **No colon reveals.** A noun phrase, a colon, then a dramatic lowercase punchline ("The result: chaos"). Write a plain sentence instead.
- **No interpretive metadiscourse.** Cut lines that step outside the subject to tell the reader what to notice or how much weight to give it ("this distinction matters", "the key point is"). If the point is clear from the facts already given, delete the aside.
- **No summary-recap endings or fake-profound kickers.** No "in conclusion", "ultimately", or a closing metaphor doing the work a plain sentence should do. End the closing section on the clearest concrete point already made, not a cute line.
- **No banned words**: delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, streamline, robust, meticulous, intricate, paramount, transformative, elevate, multifaceted, tapestry, realm, beacon, ever-evolving.

Two further rules specific to this project:

- **Every card carries an argument, not just a chronology.** The Tang card argues that the golden age was constructed retrospectively and that the "most Chinese" dynasty had frontier origins. Find the equivalent for each topic: what does a good historian actually think is at stake here.
- **State contested evidence as contested, in one clause.** The Tang card gives the census collapse from 52.9 million to 16.9 million and immediately notes that the figure reflects administrative breakdown as much as mortality. This models real historical thinking and costs one sentence. Do it wherever numbers, legendary material or traditional attributions are involved.

## Sourcing integrity

An earlier draft of the Tang card tracked closely to Mark Edward Lewis, *China's Cosmopolitan Empire*, to the level of phrasing. That cannot ship. Read widely, then write from scratch in your own sentences. Never build a card by paraphrasing one monograph or one Wikipedia article in sequence. If a specific scholar's argument is being used, name them in the text, as the Tang card names Naitō Konan for the Tang-Song transition.

Populate the `sources` array with real URLs actually consulted, and `sourceLabels` with a human-readable label for each in the form `Author · Title` or `Institution · Topic`.

## Structure

Target 600 to 950 words. Built for progressive disclosure in a 340px panel: a lead paragraph that loads by default, then expandable sections.

1. **Lead paragraph, no heading.** What the thing is, when, what it followed or belonged to, and the single claim that frames the card. Roughly 60 to 90 words.
2. **Three to six sections with `##` headings.** Headings are short and substantive ("Frontier origins", "Government", "A world capital", "The centre of gravity moves south"). Not generic ("Background", "Overview", "Legacy").
3. **A closing section, usually "Why X matters".** How the subject was remembered and used by later generations, what historians argue about it, and where it sits in the longer arc. This is the section that separates these cards from Wikipedia and it is mandatory.

For an event or person card, scale down: lead plus two or three sections plus the closing.

## Hanzi and pinyin

Every Chinese proper noun, title and institutional term gets hanzi and tone-marked pinyin **on first mention only**, in this exact format:

```
Emperor Taizong (太宗 Tàizōng, born Li Shimin 李世民 Lǐ Shìmín, r. 626–649)
the equal-field system (均田制 jūntiánzhì)
Chang'an (长安 Cháng'ān)
the Tang-Song transition (唐宋变革 Táng-Sòng biàngé)
```

Rules:

- **Simplified characters.** The corpus uses simplified. Store traditional in a separate field if it is ever added; do not mix.
- **Tone marks always** (Cháng'ān, not Chang'an or Chang1an2).
- Personal names capitalised, institutional and conceptual terms lower case (jūntiánzhì, kējǔ, jiédùshǐ).
- Reign dates as `r. 626–649` with an en dash.
- Non-Chinese names transliterated from their own language get no hanzi unless the Chinese form is the point (Talas gets 怛罗斯 Dáluósī because the Chinese transcription is how it enters the record; Nara and Heian-kyō get nothing).
- Do not gloss the same term twice in one card.

In the rendered app these should become tagged spans so the hanzi can sit lighter or reveal on hover. Write them as plain parentheses in the markdown; the build layer handles presentation.

## Wikilinks

Obsidian syntax, piped so the display text reads naturally and the target stays a stable slug:

```
[[an-lushan|rebellion of the frontier general An Lushan]]
[[chang-an|Chang'an]]
[[catalog-R_FIVE_LATER_LIANG|Later Liang]]
```

Link generously, on first mention only. Link targets that do not exist yet anyway: Obsidian renders unresolved links greyed out, which gives Wei a live queue of cards still to write.

Update the card's `related` array in frontmatter to include every slug linked, split by a comment into existing and proposed.

## The link inventory

Every card ends with a `## Link inventory` section, outside the card text, containing:

- **Already in the dataset**, listed as inline code slugs.
- **Tier 1**, new cards the article is incoherent without. Usually hubs: places, institutions, major figures.
- **Tier 2**, strongly wanted.
- **Tier 3**, eventually.

One line each explaining what the card would cover and why it earns its place. The Tang card generated 28 new cards this way. That is normal and useful: the corpus is heavy on events and named individuals and thin on places, institutions and concepts, which are exactly the nodes that make a wiki navigable.

## Images

The image pipeline already has strict standards documented in `data/image-coverage.md`. Follow them.

Prefer, in order: named historical objects, manuscript or printed pages, dated documentary photographs, identifiable sites. Political maps must be labelled as modern reconstructions with approximate boundaries. Never use generated likenesses or stock photography. A later portrait must be captioned as a later representation, and a modern photograph as a present-day or dated view.

Source from Wikimedia Commons or museum open-access programmes (the Met, Smithsonian, Cleveland Museum of Art, Getty, National Palace Museum). Verify the file record for authorship, dimensions and reuse terms before using it. Prefer 960px or 1280px preview URLs over originals, since remote hosting rate-limits large requests.

Produce a complete record in this shape:

```json
"image": {
  "src": "https://upload.wikimedia.org/.../1280px-Tang_Sancai_Camel.jpg",
  "width": 4752,
  "height": 3168,
  "alt": "A glazed ceramic camel displayed in a museum.",
  "caption": "A Tang sancai ceramic camel in Xi'an's Bell Tower Museum, photographed in 2011. An artifact offers a different kind of evidence from a written chronicle: the subjects and materials that makers chose to preserve.",
  "credit": "Gary Todd · Wikimedia Commons",
  "source": "https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tang_Sancai_Camel_(9912365146).jpg",
  "license": "CC0 1.0",
  "licenseUrl": "https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/"
}
```

`alt` is a plain physical description for screen readers. `caption` identifies the object, its date relationship to the card's subject, and what kind of evidence it is. Attribution wording required by the holding institution must be reproduced exactly.

Where a map would genuinely help (territorial extent, concurrent states, migration, campaign routes), propose one and say what it should show. Do not fabricate map images.

## Checklist before delivering

- No em dashes anywhere.
- No "not X, but Y" constructions.
- No -ing tails, no "serves as" or "represents".
- No importance puffery, weasel attribution, colon reveals, interpretive metadiscourse, summary-recap endings, or banned AI-slop words (delve, leverage, utilize, robust, meticulous, transformative and the rest of the list above).
- Every Chinese term glossed once with simplified hanzi and tone-marked pinyin.
- Lead paragraph states what the thing is, no hook.
- Every section heading is substantive.
- A closing section on historiography and afterlife.
- At least one contested figure or tradition flagged as contested.
- Wikilinks piped, first mention only, `related` array updated.
- Image record complete with alt, caption, credit, source, licence.
- Sources array populated with URLs actually read.
- Written from scratch, not paraphrased from one book.
